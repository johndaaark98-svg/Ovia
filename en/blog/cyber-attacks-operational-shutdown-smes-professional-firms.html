Cyber Attacks & Operational Shutdown: the Risk for SMEs

A cyber attack today does not just steal data: it halts work, wrecks tax deadlines and can trigger direct professional liability. Here is what really happens when systems go down.

Ovia editorial team·7 October 2026·10 min read·Regulation and security

An ordinary Wednesday morning: the owner of a professional firm switches on their computer and finds a ransom message. Files are encrypted, the practice management software is unreachable, the certified email inbox is not responding. In a few hours, a tax filing deadline expires. This is not a doomsday scenario: it is the concrete consequence of a ransomware attack, and the probability of it happening is higher than most people think.

According to Claroty's global research involving 2,000 senior executives, 56% of Italian respondents report that their organisation suffered a cyber attack with direct operational impact in the past twelve months. We are not talking about large manufacturing plants: the risk perimeter has expanded to cover any organisation that relies on its digital systems to operate. And an accountancy practice, law firm or labour consultancy relies on those systems every day, for every client.

The crucial point — and one that is often underestimated — is that cybersecurity is no longer an IT department issue. It is a prerequisite for practising a profession. This article explains what really happens when an attack shuts down a firm, what liabilities arise and how to reduce the risk without overhauling day-to-day processes.

56% of Italian organisations hit: are we really this exposed?

The answer is yes, and the data leaves no room for doubt. Claroty's "The Global State of Operational Security 2026" report — based on interviews with 2,000 senior executives worldwide, including 150 in Italy — confirms that more than half of Italian organisations have already suffered an attack with direct operational consequences within a twelve-month period.

The problem is not just frequency: it is the nature of the attacks. 33% of Italian respondents identify ransomware as one of the main risks, and the same proportion cite unsecured or unmonitored third-party access. This second figure deserves particular attention: practice management software vendors, external IT consultants, system maintenance providers — all figures that a professional firm regularly works with — represent an entry vector that is often overlooked in risk assessments.

On average, Italian organisations in the research recorded 3.11 incidents originating from third-party access in the past year. This is not an abstract problem: it is the accounting software supplier who connects remotely, or the external consultant who accesses the firm's network without two-factor authentication.

How ransomware shuts down the firm, not just the data

Ransomware is often described as a tool for "stealing data". That definition is partial and misleading. The actual mechanism works differently: the malware gains entry to systems — via a phishing email, a compromised remote connection or an unpatched vulnerability — and encrypts the files on the affected devices and any servers connected to the network. It does not copy them externally: it makes them inaccessible from within.

For a professional firm, this means the client management system is locked, shared folders are unreadable, and documents in progress are unrecoverable without the decryption key. Work stops. Not partially: completely. Systems remain down until the ransom is paid — with no guarantee of receiving the key — or until everything is restored from a clean backup, an operation that takes hours or days.

The Claroty research confirms this dynamic: in Italy, cyber incidents caused an average of 2.3 hours of operational downtime, but 10% of cases recorded outages of three days or more. For a firm with weekly tax deadlines, three days of downtime is not an inconvenience: it is a professional emergency with real consequences for clients.

A further risk factor stems from the growing adoption of artificial intelligence in operational environments: approximately two in three Italian organisations report using AI technologies in their systems, and 27% of respondents consider AI-driven cyber attacks a significant threat. AI is not only a defensive tool: it can also be used to make attacks faster, more targeted and harder to detect.

Professional liability when client data is compromised

This is the point that many firm owners underestimate: a cyber attack is not just a technical problem — it is a professional liability problem. An accountancy practice or law firm handles sensitive client data — financial positions, disputes, tax records, confidential information — and has precise obligations regarding data protection.

The GDPR requires personal data breaches to be notified to the supervisory authority (the Italian Data Protection Authority, Garante) within 72 hours of discovery, and to the affected clients when the risk to their rights is high. A ransomware attack that encrypts a firm's files almost certainly falls within this category. Failure to notify is a separate infringement, independent of the attack itself.

Added to this are the implications of professional secrecy: unauthorised access to data covered by confidentiality — even if not intentionally disclosed — may constitute a breach of professional conduct obligations. It is not necessary for the data to be published online: it is sufficient that it has been accessible to unauthorised parties.

On the regulatory front, the NIS2 Directive — transposed into Italian law — extends cyber risk management obligations to a broader range of entities, including many that until now considered themselves exempt. In parallel, the AI Act introduces transparency and human oversight requirements for AI systems used in professional contexts. Those who automate processes with AI tools without adequate governance are accumulating regulatory risk, not just technical risk. The Ovia article on the AI Act for professional firms and SMEs examines this point in depth.

⚠ Liabilities that an attack triggers automatically
  • Obligation to notify the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) within 72 hours of discovering the breach (GDPR Art. 33): failure to notify is a separate infringement, sanctionable independently of the attack.
  • Potential breach of professional secrecy if confidential client data was accessible to unauthorised third parties, even without proven exfiltration.
  • Contractual liability to clients for damages arising from missed deadlines or the compromise of their information.
  • NIS2 obligations to manage cyber risk and report significant incidents, with sanctions for entities within the directive's scope.

Operational resilience for a 5-to-20-person firm: what it really means

"Operational resilience" sounds like a term for large corporations. In reality, for a professional firm of 5–20 people, it means one thing: the ability to keep working — or to resume as quickly as possible — even after an incident. It is not a project costing hundreds of thousands of pounds: it is a set of process and configuration choices that can make the difference between 2 hours of downtime and 3 days.

The first pillar is a verified, isolated backup. Verified means periodically tested: a backup that has never been restored in a test is not a reliable backup. Isolated means physically or logically separated from the main network, so that ransomware encrypting the servers cannot also reach the backup copies.

The second pillar is access control. Knowing who can access what — and quickly revoking access when staff or suppliers change — drastically reduces the attack surface. Claroty's research highlights that in Italy 47% of respondents report IT and OT security governance that is predominantly or fully integrated, while for more than half this integration is not yet complete. In a professional firm, access governance does not require complex systems: it requires a clear policy and correctly configured tools.

The third pillar is documentation of critical processes. If systems go down, it is essential to know immediately which deadlines fall within the next 48–72 hours, how to contact clients through alternative channels and who is responsible for each recovery action. A firm that depends entirely on its software to know what to do is more vulnerable than one that also has a documented Plan B. Tools such as Ovia Second Brain allow firms to maintain an operational memory that remains accessible even in emergency scenarios.

How to reduce the attack surface without overhauling the firm's processes

The good news is that the most effective measures do not require extraordinary technology investments. They require strategic choices about what to protect and how to measure the outcome — precisely the difference between buying a tool and building a system.

The starting point is mapping critical processes: which activities, if disrupted, cause immediate harm to clients? Managing tax deadlines, accessing documents in progress, communicating via certified email (PEC), electronic signing. Once these processes are identified, assess each one: where is the most vulnerable entry point? What is the maximum tolerable downtime? Is there a manual or analogue fallback for the first few hours?

The second step is reducing the number of open doors. Every piece of software that is installed but no longer used, every remote access that was never revoked, every account with a weak password is a potentially open door. No sophisticated scanning is needed: a systematic biannual review of active access rights and software installed on the firm's devices is sufficient.

The third step concerns automation: automated processes, if designed with security in mind, reduce the attack surface by decreasing the manual actions susceptible to human error — the leading cause of cyber incidents. A system that automatically manages document collection, such as Ovia Documents, reduces the number of manual emails with unverified attachments. A system that categorises and assigns incoming emails, such as Ovia Inbox, reduces the risk of a phishing email being opened absent-mindedly by a member of staff. Automation, in this sense, is not just about efficiency: it is also a security control.

NIS2 and the AI Act: what changes in practice for professional firms

Many firm owners feel they fall outside the regulatory scope of cybersecurity: "those rules are for large companies". That perception is becoming increasingly inaccurate. The NIS2 Directive — which significantly extended the scope compared with NIS1 — and the European AI Act introduce obligations that also apply to medium and small entities, especially those that process third-party data or use AI systems in professional contexts.

NIS2 requires entities within its scope to adopt cyber risk management measures, notify significant incidents within defined timeframes and demonstrate that active security policies are in place. The fact that a professional firm is not a critical infrastructure operator does not automatically exclude it: this depends on its classification as an essential or important entity within the supply chain.

The AI Act introduces transparency and human oversight obligations for AI systems classified as high-risk — including some systems used in legal and tax contexts. Those who use AI agents to process client documents or support decision-making must be able to demonstrate that the system is controlled, auditable and compliant. On this topic, the Ovia article on Italian Law 132/2025 and client disclosure provides an up-to-date overview for professionals operating in Italy.

The convergence of operational security and regulatory compliance is the key point: these are no longer separate compliance streams to be managed in silos, but a single system in which security choices inform compliance and vice versa. Those who build this integration today gain a competitive advantage — and those who defer it accumulate risk.

Frequently asked questions

What happens to a professional firm if an attack shuts down its systems for several days?

The firm cannot access client data, the management system, emails or documents in progress. Tax or legal deadlines are missed, potentially triggering contractual liability to clients. According to Claroty's 2026 research, 10% of cyber incidents in Italy cause disruptions lasting three days or more. Without an isolated backup and a written response plan, restoration can take far longer than necessary.

56% of Italian organisations are being attacked: are professional firms really at risk?

Yes. Claroty's research among 150 Italian senior executives confirms that 56% of organisations suffered an attack with operational impact in the past year. Professional firms are attractive targets because they handle sensitive data belonging to many clients, often with less robust systems than large corporations. Not being a manufacturing business does not reduce the exposure.

How does ransomware halt operations rather than simply stealing data?

Ransomware does not necessarily exfiltrate data: it encrypts files on the systems and makes them inaccessible from within. Work stops because nobody can open documents, access the management system or use shared servers. Recovery requires either paying the ransom — with no guarantees — or restoring from a clean, isolated backup, which in serious cases can take days.

What liability does a professional have if client data is compromised by an attack?

The GDPR requires notification to the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) within 72 hours of discovering the breach, and to affected clients if the risk is high. Failure to notify is a separate, sanctionable infringement. On the professional conduct side, unauthorised access to data covered by professional secrecy may constitute a breach of confidentiality obligations, even if the data was never published.

What does 'operational resilience' mean for a firm with 5–20 people?

It means being able to keep working — or resume quickly — even after an incident. In practice: a verified backup isolated from the network, a critical deadlines register accessible without the main management system, a one-page written response plan covering the first two hours, and clarity on who has access to the firm's systems. It is not a complex project: it is a set of documented process choices.

How do I reduce the attack surface without overhauling the firm's processes?

The most effective measures require no major investment: a biannual review of active access rights (revoking unused ones), enabling two-factor authentication on all access points, periodic backup testing, and basic phishing awareness training. Automating operational processes, if designed correctly, further reduces the manual actions that represent the main attack entry vector.

Do NIS2 and the AI Act change anything for professional firms on cybersecurity?

Yes. NIS2 has expanded the scope of obligated entities and introduces requirements for cyber risk management, incident notification and documented security policies. The AI Act adds transparency and oversight obligations for AI systems used in high-risk professional contexts, including certain legal and tax applications. Those who process third-party data and use AI tools should verify their position under both frameworks.

Conclusion

Cyber risk for an Italian professional firm or SME is no longer an abstract problem that can be delegated to the IT department or an outside IT consultant. It is an operational condition: if systems go down, work stops, deadlines are missed and liabilities activate automatically. The Italian figures from Claroty's 2026 research confirm this with precise numbers.

The good news is that operational resilience does not require an extraordinary budget: it requires informed strategic choices. Knowing what to protect, testing backups, controlling access, documenting critical processes. And, when introducing automation, doing so with an integrated risk assessment — not as a separate project.

Security is not an IT cost. It is the condition for being able to work, serve clients and comply with regulations. Those who treat it as such already hold a concrete competitive advantage.
