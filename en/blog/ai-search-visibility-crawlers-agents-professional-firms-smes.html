AI Search Visibility: What to Do Right Now

Appearing in ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity answers is not automatic: it depends on who you let into your website, under what rules and with what strategy. Without a deliberate policy, you risk both disappearing from AI search and giving away data without any control.

Ovia editorial team·15 November 2026·10 min read·AI search and marketing

Online search is changing faster than most Italian professional firms and SMEs realise. This is not simply about Google tweaking its algorithm: a parallel system is emerging in which ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity answer users' questions by citing websites — without those websites necessarily being visited in the traditional sense.

For an accountancy practice, law firm, employment consultancy — or an SME that wants to be found by prospective clients — this changes the rules of the game. Ranking on Google is no longer enough: you need to understand who is accessing your website, why they are doing so and what the consequences are. And you need to do it with a strategy, not by leaving things to chance.

ⓘ Key takeaways
  • Traditional AI crawlers index websites much as Google does, but AI agents — those that act on behalf of a user — are a distinct and far more sensitive category.
  • Appearing in ChatGPT or Gemini answers depends on how your website is structured and which bots you allow in: it is not automatic and does not depend solely on traditional SEO.
  • Blanket-blocking all AI bots can make you disappear from AI search; blocking them poorly can cost you control over your own data.
  • OpenAI has launched the Business Hub in beta: companies can now claim their profile and see how often ChatGPT cites them — but claiming a profile does not guarantee visibility.
  • Organic traffic from Google is falling across many sectors: Ziff Davis reported a 37% year-on-year drop in organic referrals, and major players such as Tripadvisor are already rethinking their strategies.
  • The distinction between crawlers (which read to index) and agents (which act on behalf of the user) is the key to building a sensible policy — not a blanket block list.

Crawlers and AI agents: a distinction you cannot afford to ignore

When we talk about "AI bots" we tend to lump them all together — and that is a mistake that can prove costly. There are at least three distinct categories: training crawlers (which collect text to help AI models learn), retrieval bots for AI search (which extract content to compose real-time answers) and autonomous agents (which act on behalf of the user — searching, comparing, purchasing). Blocking the first type has very different consequences from blocking the second or third.

As Chris Green explains on Search Engine Journal, blocking an agent that is comparing products or services may not cost you a ranking position — it may cause you to vanish from the answer entirely. This matters even more for a professional firm, where a ChatGPT response to the query "who is a good accountant in Milan" can be worth as much as the top Google result.

The robots.txt file — the tool long used to tell crawlers what they can read — is voluntary. Bots comply only if they choose to. Real enforcement happens at the level of the CDN (content delivery network), the web application firewall and bot-protection services, all of which can override robots.txt rules without the team managing the site even knowing.

What is really happening to AI search visibility

The week of 10 November 2026 brought a cluster of signals that, taken together, paint a very clear picture. Google launched its third core update of the year. It changed the default model in AI Mode, switching to Gemini 4 Hydrogen — described as capable of connecting users to "a wide range of helpful links". OpenAI opened the Business Hub in beta, allowing businesses to claim their profile and see how often ChatGPT cites them and how many clicks it drives.

The most significant data, however, came from publicly listed companies. The CEO of Ziff Davis stated that organic referrals from Google fell 37% year-on-year in Q3 2026. Tripadvisor spoke openly of SEO as a "headwind to growth" extending into 2027, and increased its share of paid marketing from 43.3% to 48.8% of revenue. These are not niche players: if it is happening to them, it can happen to a professional firm or an SME that relies on organic traffic to win new clients.

Meanwhile, OpenAI was paying Cloudflare to receive priority signals about when web pages change, making crawling more efficient. Cloudflare CEO Matthew Prince compared this dynamic to "a new version of SEO". The logic is straightforward: anyone who wants their content to reach AI agents reliably needs to start thinking of their web infrastructure as a strategic asset, not a service overhead.

For a professional firm, this translates into a concrete question: is your website structured so that AI retrieval bots can read it, understand it and cite it accurately? Or is it designed only for the human visitor of the early 2000s? This is precisely where the work we do with websites designed for AI search comes in.

Who is entering your website without asking?

According to HUMAN Security's monthly report, Meta Muse and OpenAI agents generated almost 90% of AI agent requests on the sites protected by their platform in October 2026. Muse alone accounted for 59% of the total. And the overall volume of requests was approximately eight times higher than in August.

The problem is that these agents do not always identify themselves clearly. Meta itself has stated openly that Muse browses sites "as if it were your business" — meaning it appears as a normal user to analytics tools. Half of the Muse requests detected by DataDome on 10 November arrived without a verifiable digital signature. For a site that uses robots.txt to manage access, this means the rules you have set simply do not apply to these agents.

For a professional firm, this raises a practical and urgent question: do you know who is accessing your website, how often and for what purpose? If the answer is no — or if the answer is "the webmaster handles that" without you ever having seen a report — it is time to change that. Not to block everything, but to make informed decisions.

Please note: The robots.txt file is a voluntary tool — bots comply only if they choose to. Personal AI agents such as Muse do not publish a user-agent that robots.txt can recognise, so your rules do not apply to them. Real protection happens at the level of the firewall, CDN and bot-verification services — tools that must be coordinated with your SEO strategy and your legal team.

The United Airlines model: separating indexing from agent access

A concrete and very recent example of how to tackle this issue comes from United Airlines. On 11 November 2026, the company updated its terms of service to draw a clear distinction: search engines may freely index the site's public pages, but any AI agent wishing to search, book or purchase on united.com must first obtain written permission. The site's data — fares, availability, prices — cannot be used to train or fine-tune AI models.

As Search Engine Journal reports, CEO Scott Kirby summed up the rationale plainly: "I don't want to create another intermediary." United does not want third-party agents inserting themselves between the company and its customers — nor does it want its data feeding competing systems without oversight.

For a professional firm, the logic is identical. Your website can — and perhaps should — be indexed by AI search engines so that clients can find you. But an agent that accesses your documents, your diary or your contact form on behalf of a user is an entirely different matter: it has implications for professional secrecy, liability and the GDPR. The distinction between "I allow reading" and "I allow acting" is at the heart of a sensible policy, and it must be written explicitly into the site's terms of service — not left to robots.txt interpretation.

Is traditional SEO still worth doing? The honest answer

The honest answer is: yes, but it is no longer sufficient on its own, and its relative weight is shifting rapidly. Traditional SEO — optimised titles, inbound links, site speed — remains the foundation on which visibility in AI search is also built. The bots that compose Gemini or Perplexity answers draw on indexed content, and a technically poorly structured site will not be cited well by AI search engines either.

But traditional SEO was designed to drive clicks: the user reads the title on Google and visits the site. In AI search, the user receives a direct answer and may never visit the site at all. The Ziff Davis data is telling: 2.7 billion impressions in AI Overview and AI Mode in the quarter, with total clicks from Google coming in at almost ten times fewer than those impressions. Being featured in AI Overview does not drive traffic in the same way that the top Google result used to.

This does not mean abandoning SEO: it means adding a layer of AI search optimisation (often called GEO, Generative Engine Optimization). Content must be written so that it can be extracted and cited accurately: direct answers, structured data, recognisable authority. And the site must allow the right bots to read that content. For a professional firm, this also means covering the questions that prospective clients ask AI systems — "how does the forfettario flat-rate scheme work", "when is it worth setting up an SRL" — with content that answers clearly and authoritatively.

If you have already read our article on AI Overviews and the drop in clicks for professional firm websites, you already have a picture of the practical consequences. The next step is deciding what to do — and here strategy matters more than any individual tool.

AI visibility and data security: two risks to manage together

For a professional firm, the question of AI search visibility is inextricably linked to data protection. An AI agent accessing your site to gather information cannot distinguish between a public service page and a restricted area containing client documents. If the site is not configured correctly, the risk is not only one of visibility: it is a risk of breaching professional secrecy and the GDPR.

The question of professional liability in the use of AI has already been addressed in depth in the article on AI liability for professional firms and SMEs. The point here is complementary: it is not only about how you use AI within the firm, but about how external AI interacts with your website — and therefore with the data that, even indirectly, has been entrusted to you.

The answer is not to block everything — that would be counterproductive for visibility. It is to build a deliberate policy: what you permit, to whom, under what conditions and with what documentation. A policy that involves legal counsel for the terms of service, IT for the technical infrastructure, and whoever manages marketing for the implications on visibility. This is not a technical problem to delegate to the webmaster: it is a strategic decision that falls to the firm's principal or the SME's responsible manager.

Please note: An AI agent accessing your site to construct an answer may extract information you did not intend to make public — hidden text, downloadable documents, metadata. Without active firewall-level protection and an explicit policy, you have no way of knowing what has been read and by whom. For a professional firm, this is not a minor technical issue: it directly touches your duty of care to clients and your professional ethical obligations.

Frequently asked questions

How do I appear in ChatGPT or Gemini answers when someone searches for my firm?

To be cited by AI search engines, your website must be readable by AI retrieval bots and must contain clear, authoritative answers to the questions your prospective clients are asking. This requires structured data, directly written content and a technical configuration that does not block the right bots. OpenAI has also launched the Business Hub in beta, which allows you to claim your profile and monitor citations on ChatGPT.

Do AI bots like ChatGPT crawl my website without asking permission?

It depends on the type of bot. AI indexing crawlers generally declare their user-agent and comply with robots.txt, albeit on a voluntary basis. Autonomous AI agents — such as Meta Muse — can browse the site appearing as ordinary users, without following robots.txt rules. According to HUMAN Security data, in October 2026 total AI agent requests were approximately eight times higher than in August, with almost 90% coming from just two operators: Meta and OpenAI.

Should I block AI crawlers or let them through?

There is no single answer: it depends on the type of bot. Blocking crawlers that feed AI search reduces your visibility in ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity answers. Blocking autonomous agents that act on behalf of users is often the prudent choice, especially for a professional firm with confidentiality obligations. The key is to distinguish between categories and build an explicit policy — not to block or allow everything indiscriminately.

What do I need to do to be cited by Perplexity or Google AI Mode?

You need content that answers user questions directly and authoritatively, a website technically accessible to AI retrieval bots and structured data that helps AI systems identify who you are and what you do. Google has introduced Gemini 4 Hydrogen as the default model in AI Mode, described as capable of connecting users to a wide range of useful links — making a well-structured website more important than ever.

Is traditional SEO still worth doing?

Yes, but it is no longer sufficient on its own. Traditional SEO remains the technical foundation on which AI visibility is also built. But in AI search users often receive an answer without clicking through to the site: Ziff Davis recorded nearly ten times more impressions in AI Overview than total clicks from Google Search. This means you need to add AI-specific optimisation — content structure, structured data, bot access policy — without abandoning SEO fundamentals.

How do I control who accesses the content on my website?

robots.txt is the starting point, but it is not enough: it is voluntary and does not apply to agents that do not declare their user-agent. Real protection happens at the level of the web application firewall, CDN and bot-verification services. In parallel, the site's terms of service must explicitly state what automated systems are permitted to do — along the lines of what United Airlines did in November 2026. These decisions must be coordinated between whoever manages the site, the IT team and legal counsel.

Does the decline in Google traffic affect Italian professional firms too?

The phenomenon is global and structural, not limited to specific sectors. If major operators such as Ziff Davis and Tripadvisor are recording 37% drops in organic referrals and planning strategies without expecting a recovery in search traffic, Italian professional firms and SMEs must also start thinking in terms of AI visibility, not just Google rankings. The good news is that those who build a solid presence in AI search today are starting from a position of advantage.

Conclusion

The online search landscape has changed in a fundamental and irreversible way. Appearing in ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity answers is not an automatic gift from the algorithm: it is the result of deliberate choices about how the website is structured, who is let in and under what conditions. For an Italian professional firm or SME, those choices also carry a dimension of responsibility — towards clients, towards the data entrusted to you, and towards professional and regulatory obligations.

The United Airlines model — allow indexing freely, but require written permission from agents — is a concrete example of how to address the problem without sacrificing either visibility or control. You do not need to be a major airline to adopt it: you need a clear policy, written into the terms of service, applied at the technical level and reviewed regularly.

Strategy is the tool that generates value. AI — both the kind you build and the kind knocking at your website's door — is simply the instrument. Deciding who gets in, why and under what rules: that is the work worth doing right now.
