AI Vendor Lock-In: Risks for Firms and SMEs in Italy

Relying on a single public AI provider exposes professional firms and SMEs to operational outages, GDPR breaches and geopolitical risks. Here is how to build a resilient AI infrastructure.

Ovia editorial team·30 September 2026·8 min read·Regulation and security

Imagine walking into the office on a Monday morning to find that the AI tool underpinning half your workflows is unresponsive. The API is down, or worse: the provider changed its contractual terms over the weekend, or a geopolitical directive has restricted access to the service from Europe. How long before your firm genuinely grinds to a halt? For many, the answer is: a matter of hours.

This is not science fiction. Dependency on public AI providers is one of the most underestimated operational risks facing Italian professional firms and SMEs in 2026. Not because the tools are poor, but because nobody has built a strategy around them. Using ChatGPT or Claude to process confidential client documents without a backup plan is like running your entire accounting operation on a single foreign cloud with no backup — and hoping it never fails.

ⓘ Key takeaways
  • An outage or a unilateral price change by an AI provider can shut down your firm with no warning.
  • Sending confidential client data to public LLMs creates concrete risks of GDPR violations and breaches of professional secrecy.
  • Geopolitical risk is real: Europe is actively moving towards digital sovereignty precisely to reduce dependence on non-EU providers.
  • Private AI is not only for large enterprises: scalable solutions exist for small firms and SMEs alike.
  • Resilience does not mean using many tools — it means having a strategy that includes alternatives and control points.
  • Evaluating an AI provider is not about comparing benchmarks; it means analysing the contract, the registered office, the data policy and the business-continuity plan.

What really happens when the API goes down

The first question a professional firm should ask is not 'which AI model is the most accurate?' but 'what happens to my processes if this service is unavailable tomorrow morning?'. The APIs of major public providers — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google — offer high availability, but not absolute availability. Partial outages, traffic throttling and unscheduled maintenance do occur. And when your email-routing workflow, your draft-generation process or your document-analysis pipeline is built on a single external API, every interruption becomes an operational emergency.

But the risk is not purely technical. Providers can revise their pricing and terms of service with little notice: price increases, changes to usage limits, deprecation of specific models. If you have built automations on a particular model, the cost of migrating can be significant — in time, in development effort, in staff retraining. Dependency on a single external provider is technical debt that accumulates silently and is repaid all at once, invariably at the worst possible moment.

⚠ Warning signs you should not ignore
  • You have a single AI provider and no contingency plan if it becomes inaccessible.
  • Your workflows cannot function without that specific API: no emergency manual process has been documented.
  • You have never read the AI provider's contract to understand where your clients' data is processed.
  • You do not know whether the provider has its registered office and infrastructure within the EU.

GDPR and professional secrecy: the concrete risks of public LLMs

When a professional firm sends a document containing personal or confidential data to a public LLM (Large Language Model) — an AI model accessible via API or web interface — it opens up a range of regulatory problems that many practitioners underestimate. The GDPR requires that personal data be processed only by parties that have been formally appointed as data processors, with precise contractual guarantees. Most public AI providers offer these guarantees only in their paid enterprise tiers, not on standard plans.

For accountants, lawyers and employment consultants, the problem is twofold: in addition to the GDPR, professional secrecy comes into play. Client data is not merely personal data — it is information covered by professional ethical obligations that practitioners cannot transfer to third parties without explicit consent and adequate confidentiality guarantees. A public LLM that processes an employee's payslips for a client company, or the documents in a legal dispute, could potentially be used to train future models — unless the contract explicitly excludes this.

The European AI Act adds a further layer: AI systems used in high-risk contexts require technical documentation, conformity assessments and, in certain cases, registration. Anyone using an AI tool in an unstructured way — without a risk analysis — is operating in a regulatory grey area that could prove costly. The article on Italian Law 132/2025 published elsewhere on this blog examines in depth the obligation to inform clients about the use of AI within your firm.

Geopolitical risk: what if Washington pulls the plug?

The question of European digital sovereignty is not an abstract concern for policy-makers — it is a concrete operational variable for anyone running a professional firm or an SME. The leading AI providers — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google — are US companies subject to American jurisdiction. This means that, in the event of geopolitical tensions, trade sanctions or regulatory decisions, access to their services from Europe could be restricted or cut off entirely.

These are not remote hypotheticals. In recent years we have seen how US–EU dynamics can rapidly affect access to digital technologies: the debate over data transfers, the discussions around the American CLOUD Act, and the tensions over TikTok and other digital services all demonstrate that geopolitics reaches into the server room. As AI4Business notes, the answer to this risk is private AI: artificial intelligence systems that run on infrastructure controlled by the organisation itself or by trusted European providers, rather than on third-party APIs from outside the EU.

For a professional firm or a mid-sized SME, this does not necessarily mean installing physical servers on-premises. It means choosing providers with EU data centres, open-weight models hosted on a European cloud, and architectures that do not depend on a single external access point. The choice is not between 'everything public' and 'everything private' — it is between maximum exposure and managed risk.

How to reduce dependency: practical steps for firms and SMEs

Reducing dependency on a single AI provider does not require doing everything at once. It requires a progressive strategy that starts with an analysis of current risks and builds resilience step by step. The starting point is not choosing a new tool — it is mapping the processes that already rely on AI and assessing their criticality.

For every AI-dependent process, three questions must be answered: how long can it be down before causing serious impact? What would happen if the data flowing through it were exposed? Is there a manual or technological alternative already available? The answers define the priority of interventions. High-criticality processes — those handling confidential data or sitting on the critical path of daily operations — must be protected first.

1

Map your AI-dependent processesWeek 1

List every workflow that uses AI, the provider involved and its criticality to operations. Identify the data flowing through it and its sensitivity.

2

Read the contracts and verify GDPR complianceWeeks 2–3

For each provider: where is the data hosted? Is there a DPA? Is data used for training? Is the provider subject to non-EU law? Document everything.

3

Define an alternative provider for each critical processMonth 2

This does not mean duplicating everything — it means having a tested Plan B. An open-weight model on a European cloud, an alternative provider with a DPA already signed, a documented manual procedure.

4

Build a monitored multi-provider architectureMonths 3–6

Critical processes must be able to switch to an alternative provider without urgent manual intervention. Test the switchover at least once every six months.

How to assess whether an AI provider is reliable in the long term

Evaluating an AI provider for a professional firm cannot be limited to accuracy benchmarks or a product demo. The criteria that truly matter for long-term sustainability and regulatory compliance are different. The first is contractual solidity: a reliable provider clearly sets out its registered office, the applicable jurisdiction, the conditions under which service terms and pricing may change, and the data-portability guarantees upon termination.

The second criterion is transparency over data management: where data is processed, who has access to it, how long it is retained, and whether it is used to train future models. The third is financial stability and service continuity: AI start-ups with only a few months of history and no contractual commitment to continuity are a genuine risk for anyone building critical processes on top of them. As the debate on security as governance makes clear, resilience is not a technical feature but an organisational choice that is built over time.

One final element that is often overlooked: portability. If you had to switch providers tomorrow, what would it cost you in terms of data migration, automation rewrites and retraining? Providers that make it difficult to leave exercise a form of lock-in that is as risky as any technical vulnerability. Give preference to providers that use open standards and document their APIs in a way that allows substitution without overhauling your entire architecture. AI vendor dependency: the technical debt nobody wants to pay

There is a paradox at the heart of this issue: the SMEs and professional firms that adopted AI fastest are often the most exposed. They integrated powerful tools into their operational workflows, but without laying the foundations — solid contracts, documented alternatives, distributed architectures. The result is technical debt that grows every day the system works without incident, because every additional day of exclusive dependency raises the cost of exit.

The good news is that reducing this exposure does not require giving up AI altogether. It requires using it more maturely: with a strategy that separates critical processes from non-critical ones, that spreads dependency across multiple providers, that keeps confidential data on controlled infrastructure and that has a written, tested Plan B. This is precisely the kind of work that distinguishes those who use AI as a tool from those who are captive to it as a dependency.

For a professional firm, value does not lie in whichever AI model happens to be the most powerful at any given moment. It lies in having an AI infrastructure that works tomorrow, the day after and three years from now — regardless of what Washington decides, how much OpenAI changes its pricing, or which new European regulation comes into force. Strategy and security generate value. AI is the instrument.

Frequently asked questions

What happens to my firm if the AI API I use goes offline or changes its pricing overnight?

If your firm has built critical processes on a single external AI API, an outage or a unilateral change in terms can halt operations without warning. The risk covers both time (hours or days of downtime) and cost (a forced migration to unplanned alternatives). The solution is to have at least one tested alternative provider and documented emergency manual processes for every critical workflow.

Is using ChatGPT or Claude for client data safe from a GDPR perspective?

It depends on the contractual plan and how the tool is used. Standard ChatGPT and Claude plans do not automatically include a Data Processing Agreement adequate for GDPR purposes, nor do they guarantee that data will not be used to train future models. Professional firms handling confidential data must use enterprise versions with a signed DPA, EU-hosted data and explicit no-training guarantees. Without these conditions, the risk of a GDPR violation and a breach of professional secrecy is real.

What is private AI, and does it actually matter for an SME or a small firm?

Private AI refers to an artificial intelligence system running on infrastructure controlled by the organisation — its own servers, a private cloud or a certified European cloud — rather than on third-party public APIs. It is not reserved for large enterprises: solutions based on open-weight models can be hosted on a European cloud at costs accessible even for small firms. Private AI is relevant whenever the data being processed is confidential or when operational continuity is critical.

How do I avoid depending on a single AI provider?

The starting point is mapping all the processes that use AI and classifying them by criticality. For each critical process, you need to identify an alternative provider with a DPA already signed and test the switchover. Multi-provider architectures with interchangeable APIs (based on open standards) allow you to move from one model to another without rebuilding everything. The key is not to wait until the problem materialises: resilience is built when everything is working.

If the US blocks access to its AI models for Europe, what changes for me?

If you rely exclusively on US-based AI providers such as OpenAI or Anthropic, a geopolitical block — sanctions, export restrictions, regulatory decisions — could make services inaccessible from the EU without notice. This is not a hypothetical scenario: similar dynamics have already affected other digital technologies. Anyone who has built their AI infrastructure on European providers or on open-weight models hosted in the EU is insulated from this risk.

What are the concrete risks of using public LLMs with confidential client documents?

The risks are threefold: regulatory (GDPR violation if an adequate DPA is absent), ethical (breach of professional secrecy if data is transmitted to third parties without guarantees), and reputational (in the event of a data breach or the client discovering unauthorised use). Moreover, if the provider uses data to train its model, confidential information could surface in responses generated for other users.

How do I assess whether an AI provider is reliable over the long term?

The key criteria are: clear registered office and applicable jurisdiction, availability of a GDPR-compliant DPA, EU data localisation, a guarantee of no-training on client data, adequate contractual notice for price or service changes, data portability on exit, and use of open API standards. A provider that is not transparent on these points is an operational risk regardless of the quality of its model.

Conclusion

Dependency on public AI providers is the operational risk that grows quietly every day. It does not announce itself with an alarm — it shows up as a Monday morning when the firm cannot work, or as a notification from the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) about a non-compliant data transfer.

Building resilience does not mean abandoning AI. It means using it with a strategy: solid contracts, EU-hosted data, documented alternatives and processes that keep working even when the API of the moment does not respond. This is the work that distinguishes those who govern AI from those who are governed by it.
