AI Skills for Professional Firms: Beyond Use, You Need Fluency

Knowing how to click "generate" is no longer enough. For solicitors, accountants and consultants, true AI competence means being able to evaluate, verify and govern what the machine produces — before a court or a regulator does it for you.

Ovia editorial team·19 November 2026·10 min read·Strategy

Over the past few months, the landscape has become more concrete — and more risky. AI agents are entering the workflows of law firms, accountancy practices and administrative offices not as marginal support tools, but as operational actors: drafting opinion documents, analysing tax positions, managing client communications. And yet most Italian professional firms have still not answered a fundamental question: who on my team actually knows how to evaluate what AI produces?

The answer is not straightforward. 'Using' ChatGPT or another model is one thing; having the AI skills to understand when an output is reliable, when it is partial, and when it is simply wrong — despite looking perfect — is quite another. This distinction has a name: AI Fluency. And for an Italian professional firm, within the current framework of the EU AI Act and Italian Law 132/2025, it has become a matter of direct liability, not merely of efficiency.

In this article we explain what it means in practice to develop AI Fluency in a professional firm, the concrete risks faced by those who do not, and how to structure an approach that keeps the professional at the centre of judgement — and of the signature.

ⓘ Key takeaways
  • AI Fluency is not about knowing how to use a tool: it means being able to evaluate, interrogate and correct its outputs in high-responsibility professional contexts.
  • Delegating to an AI agent without checking does not transfer liability to the machine: responsibility remains with the professional who signed — or who should have checked.
  • The world's largest banks — Citibank, JPMorgan, Bank of America — have already updated their terms of service to disclaim any liability for actions carried out by third-party AI agents.
  • The EU AI Act and Italian Law 132/2025 impose obligations of human oversight of AI outputs in high-risk domains: law and tax advisory fall squarely within that category.
  • Building internal AI competence does not mean training everyone to the same level: it means identifying roles, setting verification thresholds and documenting the control process.
  • The most subtle risk is not the obvious error, but the plausible-yet-wrong output that nobody checks because it looks authoritative — the so-called automation bias.

What it really means to have AI skills in a professional firm

When discussing AI skills in a professional context, the most common risk is reducing everything to the technical dimension: which tool to use, how to write the prompt, how quickly you get a response. This view is partially correct but radically incomplete for anyone working in a regulated environment, where outputs produce legal, fiscal or financial effects on clients.

AI Fluency — a term beginning to circulate in training and academic circles to describe the ability to work with AI in a critical and informed way — is something more structured. It encompasses at least three distinct things: knowing how to ask the right questions (not just well-written prompts, but requests that reflect the real legal or tax context of the case); knowing how to recognise the limits of an output (a language model does not have access to the latest official guidance, does not know local case law, and can generate non-existent statutory references); and knowing how to document one's own supervision in a way that can be verified if challenged.

On that last point, as we have already explored when analysing the topic of AI output control, the problem is not only practical but also medico-legal: in the absence of a verification trail, any error by the AI becomes an error by the professional who signed, with no mitigating circumstances.

Who is liable if an AI agent gets a legal or tax opinion wrong?

The question many firm principals ask themselves — often implicitly — is: if the AI makes a mistake, is it my fault? Under the current regulatory framework, the answer is yes. And not as an abstract principle, but for very concrete reasons tied to the EU AI Act and Italian Law 132/2025.

The AI Act classifies as high-risk systems those used in areas that affect fundamental rights, legal relationships or significant economic decisions. Legal and tax advisory falls squarely within this perimeter. For these systems, the regulation requires active, not passive, human supervision: it is not enough to read the output — you must be able to demonstrate that you evaluated it critically. We refer readers to our in-depth piece on AI liability for professional firms for the full picture.

A signal from outside Europe is equally instructive. According to TechCrunch, Citibank — the third-largest commercial bank in the United States — has updated its terms of service to state that customers who use third-party AI tools to manage their bank accounts are entirely responsible for any transaction executed by those agents, including cases in which the tool is compromised by a cyberattack. JPMorgan, Bank of America and PNC adopted similar positions over the same period. The message is unambiguous: institutions have no intention of bearing the costs of machine errors. The same principle applies, in mirror image, to professionals: the AI does not answer for its mistakes — the person who uses it does.

A solicitor who includes in a submission a case-law citation generated by AI without verifying it, or an accountant who adopts a tax position suggested by a model without checking the latest guidance, is not delegating responsibility: they are assuming it in full, compounded by having used an unverified tool.

⚠ Warning: liability cannot be delegated to the machine
  • Citibank, JPMorgan and Bank of America have already updated their terms of service: if an AI agent executes an erroneous transaction or is hacked, the loss falls on the customer, not the bank. The same principle applies to professionals: whoever signs is liable, regardless of who — or what — produced the text.
  • Italian Law 132/2025 and the EU AI Act require that high-risk AI systems — including legal and tax advisory — be supervised by a human who can intervene, correct and document. The absence of this supervision is not an excuse: it is an aggravating factor.

AI agents move into sensitive areas: the case of legal research

The AI tool landscape has shifted rapidly from generic assistance to vertical specialisation. OpenAI recently announced that Astra for Law has reached first place in a comparative evaluation across 24 areas of law, and that access is already available to all ChatGPT Enterprise customers who request it. Tools of this calibre do not produce generic text: they produce articulated legal analyses, complete with references to statutes, precedents and doctrinal positions.

This is not necessarily a problem — it can be an enormous opportunity for firms. But it radically changes the type of competence required. When the output is generic text, the risk of error is visible even to an inattentive reader. When the output is a technically structured legal analysis, the risk of error becomes invisible to anyone without the specific preparation to recognise it. This is precisely where the distinction between 'using AI' and 'having AI Fluency' makes all the difference.

As Agenda Digitale points out, the competence that lawyers — and by extension all professionals — will need is not the ability to operate the tool, but the ability to critically evaluate its outputs in the specific context of the mandate, the client and the applicable law. This is a competence that is built, transmitted and updated: it cannot be improvised.

The invisible risk: automation bias in professional firms

There is a cognitive phenomenon that makes everything more complicated, and which is particularly dangerous in professional firms: automation bias. It is the tendency to trust automated outputs more than one's own judgement, especially when the produced text is well-structured, uses appropriate technical language and contains no obvious grammatical errors.

In a professional context, this translates into a very specific behaviour: a colleague reads the AI's output, finds it 'convincing', carries out a superficial review and signs it — or passes it to the principal who signs it without restarting the analysis from scratch. The problem is that advanced language models are trained to produce plausible text, not correct text. Plausibility and correctness are two different things, and the gap between them can mean a lost appeal or a penalty from the Italian Revenue Agency (Agenzia delle Entrate).

Building AI skills in a firm also means building resistance to automation bias: training the team to treat every AI output as a first draft from a very fast but potentially misinformed junior colleague — not as a ready-to-use opinion from a trusted expert. This shift in mindset is the core of AI Fluency, and it is also the reason why buying a tool is not enough: a process is required.

How to build the right AI skills: training, processes and governance

AI skills training in a professional firm should not be a one-off course on ChatGPT features. It should be a structured programme that answers three operational questions: what we verify, who verifies it and how we demonstrate it.

The starting point is mapping high-risk areas: those in which an AI error produces direct effects on a client — a submission, a tax return, a contract, an application. For these areas, verification is not optional and is best made explicit and documented. For low-risk areas — a courtesy reply, an internal summary, a first draft agenda — the level of control can be proportionally reduced.

The second element is tool governance: which AI tools are authorised within the firm, what data they may interact with, who has approved their use and within what scope. This is not a bureaucratic exercise: it is what distinguishes a firm that uses AI in a defensible way from one that uses it in an exposed way. For a deeper look at AI agent governance, we refer to our dedicated article on AI agent control and governance.

The third element, often overlooked, is continuous updating. Models change, regulations change, risks change. A verification process built today around a particular tool may be inadequate in six months if the tool is updated or replaced. AI Fluency is not a certificate to obtain once: it is a competence that must be kept alive through periodic reviews of procedures and outcomes.

What firms should do now: concrete priorities

The picture is clear: international financial institutions have already enshrined the principle in their contracts — if an AI agent makes a mistake, the person who used it pays. European and Italian regulation has done the same for professionals. The market for vertical AI tools is bringing advanced analytical capabilities directly into the hands of those working in high-risk domains such as law and taxation.

In this context, the priorities for an Italian professional firm are not technological: they are organisational. Before asking which tool to adopt, it is worth asking whether the team knows how to evaluate its outputs, whether a verification procedure exists and whether that procedure is documented in a way that is defensible. Structured management of internal knowledge — knowing what has been verified, by whom and when — is already a form of operational AI Fluency.

Firms that build these skills now have a real competitive advantage: they can use AI to gain time and quality, without exposing themselves to legal and reputational risks that would cancel out the benefits. Those who delay, on the other hand, risk having to manage an AI agent error without being able to demonstrate they did their part — and in 2026, that is no longer a theoretical concern.

Frequently asked questions

What is AI Fluency and how is it different from knowing how to use an AI tool?

AI Fluency is the ability to critically evaluate outputs produced by an AI system, recognise their limitations, verify their sources and document one's own supervision. Knowing how to use a tool means knowing how to get a response; having AI Fluency means knowing whether that response is reliable in the specific context — legal, fiscal, contractual — in which it will be used.

What risks does a solicitor or accountant face when relying on AI outputs without checking them?

Risks of a professional, civil and, in more serious cases, disciplinary nature. The EU AI Act and Italian Law 132/2025 impose active human supervision on AI systems used in high-risk domains such as law and tax advisory. An undetected error — a wrongly cited provision, an outdated tax position — remains the responsibility of the professional who signed, regardless of who or what produced the text.

Who is liable if an AI agent makes an error in a legal or tax opinion?

The professional who signed or who had the obligation to verify. AI has no legal personality and cannot be held accountable. This liability is not transferred by purchasing a tool or activating a service: it stays with whoever practises the profession. Banks such as Citibank, JPMorgan and Bank of America have already made an analogous principle explicit in their terms of service for customers using AI agents.

Banks are restricting AI agents — what does that have to do with my professional firm?

The movement of major banks — Citibank, JPMorgan, Bank of America, PNC — to place responsibility for errors committed by third-party AI agents onto their customers signals a broader trend: institutions are stepping back from accountability for automated systems. For a professional firm, the parallel is direct: professional bodies and courts will also assess the conduct of the professional, not that of the machine.

How do I know whether my firm has the right skills to use AI safely?

Start with three questions: does the team know how to verify the statutory sources cited in an AI output? Is there a written procedure defining who checks what and how they document it? Have clients been informed of the use of AI tools in services that affect them? If the answer to any of these is no, the risk is real and can be reduced with targeted organisational steps.

How should a firm's team be trained in the responsible use of AI?

Effective training is not about tool features, but about critically evaluating outputs: how to verify a cited provision, how to spot a well-constructed hallucination, how to document one's supervision. It should be complemented by written procedures that set different verification thresholds for outputs of different risk levels, and by periodic reviews whenever tools or regulations change.

What is automation bias and why is it dangerous in a professional firm?

Automation bias is the tendency to trust automated outputs more than one's own judgement, especially when the text is well-structured and technically plausible. In a professional firm it manifests as superficial review of AI outputs that appear correct but contain non-obvious errors — wrong statutory references, outdated tax positions, non-existent case-law citations. It is the most subtle and hardest risk to manage without explicit processes.

Conclusion

AI Fluency is not an optional competence for Italian professional firms: it is the minimum condition for using AI safely in areas where errors have real consequences for clients and for the practice. Technology is advancing rapidly — tools such as Astra for Law are bringing advanced analytical capabilities directly into the legal workflow — but the liability framework does not change: whoever practises the profession signs, and whoever signs is responsible.

The clearest signal comes from the most powerful institutions in the global financial system, which have already written into their contracts what was previously implicit: AI agents make mistakes, and the problem belongs to whoever used them. For an Italian professional firm, building internal AI skills is therefore not merely a matter of efficiency — it is a matter of professional resilience.

The next step is concrete: map where AI is already present in the firm's processes, define who verifies what and how they document it, and train the team to critically evaluate outputs. Those who do this now build a defensible competitive advantage. Those who delay are quietly accumulating an exposure that will, sooner or later, become a visible problem.
