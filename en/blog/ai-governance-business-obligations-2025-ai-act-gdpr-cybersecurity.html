AI Governance for Businesses: 2025 Obligations

AI Act, GDPR, Platform Directive, Italian Law 132/2025: professional firms and SMEs in Italy are caught between overlapping obligations. Simply "using AI" is not enough — you need a governance strategy that sets out what is monitored, for how long, who is accountable, and how everything is kept secure.

Ovia editorial team·17 November 2026·8 min read·Regulation and security

If you use AI in your firm or company — even just ChatGPT to draft an email or a tool to analyse documents — you are already inside a web of overlapping obligations. The European AI Act requires logs and monitoring for high-risk systems. The GDPR restricts how long those logs can be kept. The Platform Directive, in force from 2 December, compels anyone using algorithms in employment decisions to explain how they work. And in the meantime your staff are using AI tools you have not approved, uploading data you should not be sharing with any external server.

The problem is not any single regulation: it is that these rules do not speak the same language, and none of them tells you exactly what to do on Monday morning. This article does. It takes each obligation, translates it into operational terms for an Italian professional firm or SME, and shows where to start — without requiring you to become an expert in digital law.

ⓘ Key takeaways
  • The AI Act requires logs and continuous monitoring for high-risk AI systems: whoever operates them must retain the records and demonstrate compliance.
  • The GDPR imposes the data-minimisation principle even on logs: retaining them for too long exposes you to sanctions from the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante).
  • From 2 December, the Platform Directive obliges anyone using algorithms in employment decisions to guarantee transparency and explainability.
  • Shadow AI — the unauthorised use of AI tools by staff — is the most underestimated risk in Italian professional firms.
  • AI-enabled cyber attacks represent a significant financial risk even for SMEs: a substantial share of organisations hit in Italy suffer severe and lasting damage.
  • An internal AI-use policy is not red tape: it is the instrument that separates those who are accountable from those who face stacked penalties.

What obligations apply if you use AI in your firm or company

The honest answer is: it depends on what you use and how you use it. The European AI Act classifies AI systems by risk level. High-risk systems — which include tools used in legal, tax, HR and essential-services contexts — must meet precise requirements: technical documentation, an activity log, human oversight, and robustness testing. Whoever deploys such a system is the «deployer» — that is you, even if the system was built by a third-party supplier.

Alongside the AI Act, the GDPR continues to apply whenever an AI system processes personal data — which is almost always, given that client, staff and supplier data almost invariably qualifies as personal data. And then there is Italian Law 132/2025, which introduced a duty to inform clients when a professional uses AI in a significant way in the delivery of a service. For a deeper look at this specific point, read our article on AI liability for professional firms and SMEs.

In short: there is no professional firm or SME that uses AI and does not fall under at least one of these regulatory frameworks. The question is not «am I obliged?» but «which obligations genuinely apply to me and in what order should I address them?».

Logs and data retention: the tension between the AI Act and the GDPR

One of the most delicate — and least practically discussed — issues concerns AI system logs. The AI Act requires that high-risk systems maintain automatic records of the operations performed, sufficient to reconstruct what the system did and when. These logs exist to demonstrate compliance during an inspection or following an incident.

The problem is that retaining those logs for too long can breach the GDPR, which imposes the principles of data minimisation and storage limitation: personal data may not be kept longer than necessary for the purpose for which it was collected. If your logs contain — even indirectly — identifying data relating to clients or employees, every unjustified extra day of retention is an exposure to the risk of a sanction from the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante).

The solution is not to choose between the two obligations but to design a differentiated retention policy: anonymous or pseudonymised technical logs kept for the period required by the AI Act; logs containing personal data subject to limited retention with automatic deletion. This calls for a deliberate decision, not a default configuration left to your software supplier.

From 2 December: what changes with the Platform Directive

From 2 December the European Directive on platform work is fully applicable, having been transposed into Italian law. The legislation obliges anyone using algorithms in decisions affecting workers to guarantee transparency and explainability: not just large gig-economy platforms, but any company that uses automated systems to assess performance, assign tasks, set schedules or take disciplinary decisions.

In practice: if you use HR software with AI components to manage leave, evaluate performance or monitor attendance, you must be able to explain to workers how the system works and what variables influence the decisions that affect them. You must also ensure that human intervention is always possible before an automated decision produces significant effects.

For a professional firm with employees or collaborators, this means reviewing supplementary contracts, updating data-processing notices and — if you use project-management tools with built-in AI — checking whether they fall within the scope of the Directive. The Italian National Labour Inspectorate (Ispettorato Nazionale del Lavoro) has already announced targeted inspections: the risk of stacked penalties (Garante + Inspectorate) is real.

Important: Anyone using algorithms in employment decisions without adequate transparency risks sanctions from both the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante, under the GDPR) and the National Labour Inspectorate (under the Platform Directive). The two sets of proceedings are independent and can be imposed simultaneously.

Shadow AI: the risk you cannot see but that affects you

Shadow AI refers to all the AI tools your staff use without your knowledge or authorisation. A trainee who uploads a contract to ChatGPT for a summary. A secretary who uses an AI translation tool to reply to a foreign client. An accountant who queries an AI assistant using a client's balance-sheet data.

None of these behaviours is necessarily malicious. But all of them involve transmitting data to third-party servers — often outside the EU — without an adequate legal basis, without a DPA, and without the client having been informed. For a firm with a professional secrecy obligation, this is a double breach: of the GDPR and of the professional code of conduct.

The subject is well covered in the context of AI policies for businesses: without written and communicated rules, every employee makes an individual choice. The result is that your firm's AI governance depends on the common sense of each individual colleague — which is precisely the opposite of governance. An internal policy does not need to be a 40-page document: it needs to state clearly which tools are approved, which data must never be uploaded to external systems, and what happens if the rule is broken.

AI-enabled cybersecurity threats: the real risk for an Italian SME

Cyber attacks are no longer exclusively a large-company problem. AI-enabled attacks are hitting Italian professional firms and SMEs with increasing frequency, at costs that many organisations find difficult to absorb: automatically generated phishing with personalised tone and context, voice deepfakes impersonating the owner, ransomware that analyses systems before striking at the most vulnerable moment.

For a professional firm, the most common vector remains compromised email — a subject we have already examined when analysing BEC fraud and advanced phishing. But AI governance introduces a new vector: poorly configured internal AI systems, with excessively broad access rights, can become an entry point for anyone seeking to exfiltrate sensitive data or disrupt the firm's operations.

The answer is not to give up on AI. It is to build a minimal-access architecture: every AI tool should have access only to the data it needs for its specific task, not to the firm's entire archive. This principle — known as «least privilege» — is already implicitly required by the AI Act for high-risk systems and by the GDPR for any automated processing.

Where to start: AI governance without becoming a regulatory expert

AI governance is not built by starting from the regulation. It is built by starting from three operational questions: which AI systems am I (or are my colleagues) using? What data do they process? Who is responsible if something goes wrong? The answers to these three questions are worth more than any AI Act training course.

The next step is to choose what to automate deliberately, not to chase the latest trend. A professional firm that automates document management and follow-up reminders on a controlled system, with internal logs and restricted access, is already far more compliant — and far more secure — than a firm running ten different AI tools without knowing where the data goes.

Finally, governance requires a clear point of accountability: one person (not necessarily a technical expert) who knows which AI systems are in use, can update or deactivate them, and is answerable for the decisions made. In SMEs and professional firms, that person is almost always the principal. Delegating this responsibility without any safeguards is the fastest way to find yourself exposed to penalties that no adviser had flagged in advance.

Frequently asked questions

What obligations apply if I use AI in my firm or company?

It depends on the type of AI system and the data it processes. As a general rule: the AI Act imposes logs, human oversight and documentation for high-risk systems (including many tools used in legal, tax and HR contexts); the GDPR applies whenever personal data is processed; Italian Law 132/2025 requires you to inform clients about your use of AI. In most cases, a professional firm or SME will fall under at least two of these regimes simultaneously.

How long must I retain AI system logs under the GDPR and the AI Act?

The AI Act requires logs to be kept for the entire operational lifetime of the system in order to guarantee traceability. The GDPR, by contrast, requires that personal data contained in logs is not retained for longer than necessary. The solution is a differentiated policy: anonymous or pseudonymised technical logs retained long-term; logs containing personal data subject to limited retention and automatic deletion.

What are the risks if I have no policy on AI use by my colleagues?

The risk is that your colleagues will use unapproved AI tools and upload sensitive client data to third-party servers — often outside the EU — without a legal basis or a DPA. For a firm with a professional secrecy obligation, this can constitute a breach of both the GDPR and the professional code of conduct. Without a written policy, liability falls on the firm's principal, not the individual colleague.

What changes from 2 December for companies using algorithms in employment decisions?

From 2 December the European Platform Directive is fully applicable. Anyone using algorithms to assess performance, assign tasks, set schedules or take disciplinary decisions must provide workers with transparency about how the system works and what variables influence the decisions. Human intervention before any significant automated decision is also required. Shortcomings in this area expose you to sanctions from both the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) and the National Labour Inspectorate.

How do I manage shadow AI — AI tools used by employees without my knowledge?

The first step is to map what your colleagues actually use, via a questionnaire or a direct conversation. Then define a list of approved tools (with verified DPAs), identify the categories of data that must never be uploaded to external systems, and communicate the policy in writing, obtaining a read-receipt confirmation. You do not need to ban everything: you need to make clear what is and is not permitted, and to document that communication.

Are AI-enabled cyber attacks a genuine risk for an Italian SME?

Yes. In Italy, many organisations hit by AI-enabled cyber attacks suffer significant financial damage that is difficult to absorb. The most common vector remains compromised email, but poorly configured internal AI systems — with excessively broad access rights — can become entry points for data exfiltration. The least-privilege principle (each AI tool accesses only the data it needs) is the most effective and least costly technical measure to implement.

Where do I start to build AI governance without becoming a regulatory expert?

Start with three questions: which AI systems are in use (including shadow AI), what personal data do they process, and who is responsible if something goes wrong? The answers build a map of real risk. From there, write an internal policy, verify DPAs with suppliers, and appoint an internal AI lead — not necessarily a technical expert, but someone who has visibility over all active systems and can update or deactivate them.

Conclusion

AI governance is not a future problem: it is a present obligation, already enforceable in Italy through the AI Act, the GDPR, Italian Law 132/2025 and, from 2 December, the Platform Directive. Professional firms and SMEs waiting for the «definitive guidance note» are accumulating exposure, not time.

The starting point is not the regulation: it is knowing what is running in your systems, who has access, where the data goes and who is accountable for the decisions made. That clarity is worth more than any AI tool — and it is exactly the strategy that separates those who benefit from AI from those who bear its risks.
