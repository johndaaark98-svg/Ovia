AI Email Fraud: Protecting Your Firm from BEC Attacks

AI-powered BEC and phishing attacks can no longer be spotted with the naked eye. For a professional firm or Italian SME, the risk is not just technical — it's a threat to cash flow, reputation and direct liability towards clients.

Ovia editorial team·16 November 2026·8 min read·Regulation and security

An email arrives from a long-standing supplier: the tone is correct, there are no grammatical errors, the logo looks perfect and the signature is right. It asks you to update the IBAN for the next payment. You've received messages like this dozens of times. The difference today is that no human being may have written it.

AI-powered email fraud — in particular so-called BEC (Business Email Compromise) and next-generation phishing — is reshaping the risk profile for professional firms and Italian SMEs. Not because the technology has become more complex, but because the tell-tale signs we used to identify a scam have disappeared. This article explains what is happening, what you are concretely exposed to, and — most importantly — what you can do starting tomorrow morning.

ⓘ Key takeaways
  • AI eliminates the grammatical and stylistic errors that were once the main indicators of a fraudulent email.
  • A BEC attack does not breach your IT systems: it convinces someone in your firm to do something — make a transfer, change an IBAN, send documents.
  • The FBI has warned that AI agents can now compromise a system in under three hours from initial access.
  • The most effective defence is not software: it is a verifiable internal procedure — a phone call to a known number before authorising any change to bank details.
  • A single non-compliant outbound communication can be costly: the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) sanctioned a company over a promotional call lasting just 46 seconds.
  • Security and documented procedures are not a cost: they are a measurable competitive advantage, reducing direct losses and protecting your reputation with clients.

What is BEC fraud and how does AI make it almost invisible?

Business Email Compromise (BEC) is a fraud that does not attack your servers: it attacks trust. The criminal impersonates someone you know — your accountant, a supplier, a client, the firm's principal — and asks you to take an action: update an IBAN, send a confidential document, approve an urgent payment. No system needs to be breached; a person simply needs to be persuaded.

Until a few years ago, these attacks were recognisable by their mistakes: mangled Italian, an odd tone, poor formatting, email addresses with suspicious characters. Today, as documented by Agenda Digitale, AI generates flawless text in any language, replicates a person's writing style by analysing their public emails or previous communications, and constructs contextually coherent messages — referencing a real project, an actual meeting, a genuine deadline.

The result is that the visual filter — 'this email looks odd' — no longer works. Even an experienced and careful professional can be deceived, not through carelessness, but because the message is objectively indistinguishable from the real thing. This changes everything: the response cannot be 'pay more attention' — it must be procedural.

How long does an attacker need to cause damage? Under three hours

One of the most underestimated aspects is the speed at which an attack can escalate. Cybersecurity360 reports an FBI warning: AI agents can now gain significant access to a system in under three hours from initial entry. That means if a colleague in your firm clicks a malicious link at 9 a.m., by midday the attacker may already have extracted documents, credentials and sensitive client data.

For a professional firm, this has direct implications: the data you handle every day — tax returns, financial statements, contracts, litigation files — is extremely valuable to anyone seeking to blackmail a business or sell information on the dark web. Human reaction time — noticing the incident, calling the IT provider, blocking access — is structurally slower than the speed of an automated attack.

This does not mean you need to build a security operations centre. It means that incident response procedures must be written down, tested and known by all staff before anything happens — not improvised in a moment of panic.

The procedures that genuinely protect a professional firm

The good news is that the most effective defence against BEC fraud does not require a large IT budget. It requires written procedures that are actually followed. The basic principle is straightforward: any request involving a financial action or the sharing of sensitive data must be verified through a channel other than email — a phone call to a known number, not the one listed in the suspicious message.

This applies in particular to changes in bank details, urgent payment requests, and the sending of confidential documents outside normal procedures. As Agenda Digitale analyses in its in-depth piece on AI-powered phishing, an email that looks authentic is never sufficient grounds to authorise a critical action. Verification must be the default, not the exception.

On the technical side, there are domain authentication tools — in particular DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting & Conformance) and BIMI (Brand Indicators for Message Identification) — that make it considerably harder for an attacker to send emails that appear to come from your domain. DMARC is a protocol that specifies what to do with emails that fail authentication checks; BIMI adds the display of your official logo in compatible email clients, making the real sender's identity visible. These are not silver bullets, but they raise the bar in a measurable way. For a firm handling sensitive communications with clients and public bodies, configuring them is a concrete priority — not an optional extra.

1

The two-channel ruleFor every critical request

Any request to change an IBAN, make an urgent payment or send confidential documents must be verified by phone on a known number, regardless of how authentic the email appears.

2

List of actions that cannot be authorised by emailWritten procedure

Put in writing which actions can never be authorised by email alone: changes to bank details, transfers above a defined threshold, sending client files to third parties.

3

Configure DMARC on your domainOne-off technical task

Ask your email provider or IT technician to configure DMARC in 'reject' mode: fraudulent emails attempting to use your domain are blocked before they reach the recipient.

4

Basic staff trainingAt least once a year

No lengthy course is required: 30 minutes showing real-life AI BEC examples and reinforcing the procedures is enough. The goal is not to create experts, but to break the automatic habit of 'click and act'.

5

Written incident response planHave it ready before anything happens

Who do you call if you suspect an attack? Who blocks access? Who notifies clients? These questions must have written answers — with names and numbers — available to everyone in a known location.

Are you liable to your client if you fall victim to a BEC scam?

It is the question nobody wants to ask, but every firm principal must already have answered. The answer is: it depends on how you behaved beforehand. If you adopted documented procedures, communicated them to staff and implemented reasonable technical measures (such as DMARC), you have built a defensible position. If you did nothing and a colleague transferred funds on the instructions of a fraudulent email, your exposure — contractual, professional and potentially in damages — is real.

For professional firms, the issue intersects with the GDPR: if the fraud involved a breach of clients' personal data (extracted documents, files accessed by unauthorised third parties), you are obliged to notify the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) within 72 hours and, in certain cases, to inform the clients themselves. Incident management is not optional: it is a regulatory obligation with strict deadlines. You can explore the topic of AI liability and regulation further in our article on AI liability for professional firms and SMEs.

There is also a frequently overlooked collateral risk: outbound communications. The case documented by FederPrivacy is telling: Business School Srl received a significant sanction from the Garante (Provision no. 642 of 25 September 2026) for a single 46-second promotional call to the wrong number, described as an operator error. The Garante did not accept this explanation as a defence for the data controller's liability. The message is clear: governing communications — not just inbound ones, but also those your firm sends out — is a regulatory priority, not merely a technical matter.

Warning: A commercial communication sent to an unauthorised number — even by mistake, even for 46 seconds — can result in a sanction from the Garante. The data controller's liability is not extinguished by a human-error defence. Governing outbound communication flows (email, certified email/PEC, phone calls, SMS) with traceable procedures and tools is an integral part of privacy compliance, not a luxury.

Security as a competitive advantage: what firms and SMEs can do today

There is a widespread narrative that treats cybersecurity as a necessary but tedious cost. It is wrong. For a professional firm or Italian SME, demonstrating to clients that their data is safe is a genuine differentiator — particularly in a market where trust is increasingly the key battleground.

Anti-fraud procedures, the technical configuration of your email domain, the incident response plan: none of this exists solely to prevent losses. It exists so that you can tell clients, with documentation in hand, exactly how their information is protected. In a context where the EU AI Act and Italian Law 132/2025 raise diligence expectations for professionals using automated systems, documented procedures are also a professional ethics requirement.

The starting point is not a technology tool: it is an honest assessment of which communication flows in your firm currently lack verification procedures. Inbound emails containing critical requests, outbound communications to clients and suppliers, certified email (PEC) management, triaging of urgent matters — these are all areas where structured email management can reduce exposure to human error and guarantee traceability. Traceability, in the event of a dispute, is your first line of defence.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell whether an email that appears to be from my supplier is genuine or an AI scam?

You can no longer do so by looking at the email alone: AI generates flawless text, replicates the sender's style and references real details. The only reliable method is to verify through a different channel — call the supplier on a number you have saved yourself, not the one shown in the message. If the request concerns bank details or payments, a confirmation call must be mandatory by procedure, not optional.

What is BEC fraud and how does it work with artificial intelligence?

BEC stands for Business Email Compromise: it is a scam that does not breach your IT systems, but convinces someone in your firm to take an action — change an IBAN, make a transfer, send confidential documents. With AI, the fraudulent message is written perfectly, uses the real sender's tone and style, and references plausible contexts. The visual signal we have always relied on — 'this email looks odd' — no longer exists.

What procedures should I put in place to protect my firm from email fraud?

The three most effective measures are: a written rule requiring telephone verification for any critical request (payments, IBAN changes, sending sensitive documents); DMARC technical configuration on your email domain, which prevents fraudsters from impersonating you; and a written response plan describing what to do in the first hours after a suspected attack. These measures do not require large budgets — they require a decision and discipline.

If I fall victim to a BEC scam, am I liable to my client?

It depends on what you did beforehand. If you have documented procedures, trained staff and reasonable technical measures in place, you have a defensible position. If you did nothing, your contractual and professional exposure is real. If the fraud involved a breach of clients' personal data, you are required by the GDPR to notify the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) within 72 hours, regardless of who caused the incident.

Does AI render traditional anti-phishing systems based on spotting errors obsolete?

Yes, largely. Traditional spam filters look for known patterns — suspicious links, known malicious senders, keywords — but do not detect AI-generated text that is syntactically and stylistically correct. This does not mean filters are useless, but they are no longer sufficient on their own. Defence must shift towards human procedures and technical domain authentication (DMARC), not just automated message detection.

What are DMARC and BIMI, and are they really useful for a professional firm?

DMARC is a technical protocol that tells mail servers what to do with emails that claim to come from your domain: ignore them, quarantine them or block them. BIMI adds the display of your official logo in compatible email clients, making the difference between your real address and an imitation visible. For a firm that sends communications to clients via certified email (PEC) and standard email, configuring DMARC is a concrete and relatively low-cost measure that raises the barrier for anyone trying to impersonate you.

How quickly can an AI cyberattack compromise my system?

According to an FBI warning reported by Cybersecurity360, AI agents can now gain significant access to a system in under three hours from initial entry. This means the window between the first click on a malicious link and the potential exfiltration of data is drastically shorter than average human reaction time. That is precisely why the incident response plan must be written down and known in advance — not pieced together in the heat of an emergency.

Conclusion

AI-powered email fraud is not a future threat: it is already hitting professional firms and Italian SMEs, with direct financial losses and regulatory consequences that can stem from a single poorly handled communication. The answer is not to buy more technology: it is to decide which procedures to adopt, document them, and ensure they are followed.

The honest starting point is this: run through the checklist in this article and count how many items you can tick today. If the number is small, that is not a failure — it is a work plan. Every safeguard you add concretely reduces your exposure and strengthens the trust your clients place in you.

Security and strategy are not two separate concepts: a well-built defence is also an argument to put on the table with clients. Those who protect others' data with documented procedures have something more to offer than those who rely on luck and a decent antivirus.
