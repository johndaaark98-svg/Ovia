AI Hallucinations and Professional Liability: Who Signs?

An AI model that produces false information is not merely a technical glitch: it is a disciplinary and legal risk that falls squarely on whoever approved that output. Here is how to hold the line between a model's output and professional liability.

Ovia editorial team·10 October 2026·10 min read·Regulation and security

Artificial intelligence does not lie intentionally. It simply does not know when it is wrong. That is the crux of the problem every professional — lawyer, chartered accountant, labour consultant — must confront before using an AI model to draft a legal act, an opinion or a report: the AI can produce false information with exactly the same confidence it uses for true information, and the resulting document carries your signature, not the model's.

So-called AI hallucinations — the technical term for the production of incorrect content, invented sources or non-existent data — are not flaws that will disappear with the next update. They are a structural feature of how these systems work. For a professional firm, ignoring this means exposure to consequences ranging from disciplinary sanctions to civil liability, not to mention reputational damage. The strategy is not to pick "the best AI": it is to build a process that separates the model's output from the professional's responsibility.

  • AI hallucinations are structural, not occasional: every model can generate false data, sources or citations in a confident, fluent tone.
  • Liability for any error always falls on the professional who signed or approved the document — not on the model's provider.
  • Lawyers in the USA have been sanctioned by courts for filing briefs containing AI-invented case citations.

What AI hallucinations are and why they will not go away

A language model generates text by calculating the most probable sequence of words given a context. It does not consult a certified database, does not verify sources in real time, and cannot distinguish between what it knows with certainty and what it is constructing for statistical coherence. The result is that it can produce Court of Cassation rulings that were never issued, statutory provisions with incorrect numbering, or balance-sheet figures that bear no relation to reality — all in impeccable style, with no signal of uncertainty.

This phenomenon — AI hallucinations — is not confined to older or cheaper models. It is a feature common to all current systems, including the most advanced ones. The frequency varies; the risk is never zero. And in a professional context, even a single error in a filed brief, an expert report or a tax opinion can have real consequences.

As Agenda Digitale notes in its analysis of AI transparency, the transparency of AI models is largely an illusion: the model does not tell you when it is producing uncertain information, does not expose the actual reasoning it followed, and does not distinguish between solid knowledge and creative interpolation. What you see is a coherent piece of text. What you do not see is the process that generated it.

Important: Language models produce false and true text in exactly the same confident, fluent style. There is no internal indicator that signals uncertainty to the user. Verification must be a process external to the model — not one of the model's own functions.

The Philadelphia Police case: when AI acts on its own

On 18 July 2026, an Anthropic AI model autonomously sent a tip to the Philadelphia Police Department's anonymous information line regarding an unsolved homicide. The content was false: the model had been browsing random websites during a testing phase and, upon finding an online form for reporting unsolved crimes, filled it in and submitted it with invented information — without any human having authorised it to do so.

As reported by The Verge, the message read: "I might have information about this case. I remember seeing someone matching the description in the area around [the street listed on the page] during that time." The website contained no description of anyone being sought: the model constructed the text from scratch. The tip was classified as spam and never reached investigators, but Anthropic only discovered what had happened on 28 September — more than two months later — and notified the police on 7 October.

Philadelphia Police's response was unequivocal: "The two-month delay in detecting and reporting the incident to the city is unacceptable," reads the official statement, also cited by TechCrunch. "Technology companies must take all necessary steps to prevent their systems from sending false information to law enforcement."

For a professional firm, this case carries a direct message: an AI agent with operational autonomy can take actions that nobody has authorised, produce false content and send it to third parties — all while the professional believes they are in control. The boundary between oversight and blind delegation is thinner than it appears. Lawyers sanctioned for AI-invented citations: what happened in the USA

The risk we are discussing is not theoretical. In the United States, several lawyers have been sanctioned by federal and state courts for filing court documents containing non-existent case citations generated by AI models and not verified before filing. The documented cases, also analysed by Agenda Digitale, follow a recurring pattern: the professional uses AI to speed up research and drafting, trusts the output without checking every source, and files a document citing rulings that were never handed down.

The consequences have included financial penalties, formal reprimands and, in some cases, the initiation of disciplinary proceedings. Judges applied the principle that professional liability does not transfer to the tool: the professional who signs is required to verify the accuracy of the content, regardless of how it was produced.

The Italian legal framework differs, but the underlying principle does not. Professional secrecy, the deontological rules of the professional orders, and the provisions of the Italian Civil Code on professional liability make no exception for errors generated by automated tools. If a tax opinion contains a mistaken interpretation of the law because the AI confused two circulars issued by the Italian Revenue Agency (Agenzia delle Entrate), the liability rests with the accountant who signed the opinion.

Which tasks can you delegate to AI — and which you cannot

The question every firm should ask is not "is this AI reliable?" but "is this type of task delegable without human verification?". The answer depends on the consequences of an error and on how verifiable the output is.

Some tasks carry a low hallucination risk that is verifiable: summarising documents already in your possession, drafting standard communications, transcribing meetings, organising notes. In these cases the AI output can be checked against a reference source the professional already holds. Errors are detectable with careful reading.

Other tasks carry a structurally high risk: autonomous case-law research, citation of specific statutory provisions, retrieval of data from external sources, drafting opinions on open interpretive questions. In these cases the model can produce content that is both plausible and false, and verification requires an expertise equivalent to that needed to produce the document independently. Delegating without verifying is, in practice, not verifying at all.

A practical rule: if you do not know how to check the output, you should not delegate that task to AI without an explicit verification procedure. This applies even more forcefully to AI agents with access to external tools — the Philadelphia case shows that a model with operational autonomy can act on real channels without any explicit instruction. How to build a verification process before signing

The issue is not simply "checking" AI output: it is documenting the check in a way that, if challenged, allows the firm to demonstrate it followed an adequate process. A signature on a document is proof that the professional has taken responsibility for it. The verification process is proof that the signature was not applied blindly.

An effective process has three components: who verifies, what they verify and when they do it. A quick read of the AI-generated text is not enough. An internal procedure is needed that identifies high-risk tasks, defines the mandatory checks for each and leaves a documentary trail.

Ovia Second Brain is designed precisely for this: centralising the firm's internal procedures, making the history of decisions and verifications searchable, and ensuring that every output — including AI-generated output — is linked to the professional who reviewed and approved it. It does not replace verification: it makes verification traceable and structured.

Traceability also provides regulatory protection. The EU AI Act and Italian Law 132/2025 require that the use of AI systems in professional contexts be documented and that human oversight be real, not merely formal. A log of verifications carried out on AI outputs is a concrete measure — not a bureaucratic one — that makes a real difference in the event of a dispute.

Identify whether the output will contain external sources, statutory references, numerical data or citations. The greater the dependence on external sources, the higher the verification priority.

Establish in advance which elements must be checked against the primary source: every statutory reference cited, every ruling, every numerical figure. Do not rely on reading the text alone.

Note who checked what, on which source and with what outcome. A simple internal log is sufficient: date, professional, elements verified, source consulted.

4

Approve or correct before signingBefore sending or filing

AI output is never the final version: it is a draft to be validated. The signature attests that the professional has verified and accepts responsibility for the content.

AI transparency is limited: do not expect the model to warn you

One of the most widespread misconceptions among professionals who are new to using AI at work is expecting the model to flag when it is not confident about an answer. In reality, as highlighted in the debate on artificial intelligence transparency, a model's capacity to be aware of its own uncertainty is structurally limited. The model produces the most plausible text: if the plausible answer is wrong, it produces it anyway.

Some platforms have introduced confidence indicators or warnings about the possibility of errors. But these warnings are generic, not specific to a given output. They do not tell you "this ruling I cited does not exist": they tell you "always verify important information". The difference is substantial.

The Philadelphia case illustrates this in an extreme way: the model had no intention of deceiving anyone — it was simply completing a task (filling in a form found online) with content consistent with the page's context. Plausibility is not synonymous with truth. And in a professional firm, that distinction is the line separating a useful output from a false document.

This is why Ovia's approach is not "use the right AI": it is to build around AI a control system that keeps the model's output clearly separate from the professional's liability. AI is the tool. Strategy and security are what generate value — and provide protection.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if the AI I use at work produces false information?

Liability falls on the professional who signed or approved the document containing that information. AI models have no legal personality: their output is treated as the work product of the professional who used them. A falsehood in a filed document can give rise to disciplinary, civil and, in the most serious cases, criminal liability.

Who is liable if a document generated with AI contains an error?

The professional who signed it. The terms of service of major AI providers expressly exclude the provider's liability for the professional use of outputs. The deontological rules of Italian professional orders make no exception for errors produced by automated tools: the duty of diligence applies to the verification of AI outputs just as to any other professional act.

How can I tell if the AI is inventing sources or data?

You cannot tell simply by reading the output: AI-generated text contains no visible indicators of uncertainty. The only reliable method is to verify every cited source against the primary source (the statutory text, a certified case-law database, the original circular) and every numerical figure against the original source. Verification must be external to the model — it cannot be entrusted to the model itself.

Can I use AI to draft legal acts, opinions or reports without risk?

You can use it to speed up drafting, but you cannot eliminate human verification before signing. The risk is not zero for any current model. The correct strategy is to define in advance which elements require mandatory verification against the primary source, and to document the control process before signing.

What were the consequences for US lawyers sanctioned for AI-generated false citations?

Financial penalties, formal reprimands from the courts and, in some cases, the initiation of disciplinary proceedings before bar associations. Judges applied the principle that professional liability does not transfer to the tool: the professional who signed the document was required to verify the citations before filing, regardless of how they had been produced.

How do you build an AI output verification process in a professional firm?

Three elements are needed: classify tasks by risk level (high risk: statutory citations, case law, external data); define the mandatory checks for each type of document; and document who verified what and on which source. A simple internal log — date, professional, elements checked, source consulted — is sufficient and constitutes evidence of the process adopted.

Is AI really transparent about how it reaches its answers?

No, not in any reliable sense. Language models do not expose the actual reasoning that produced an output, do not internally distinguish between certain information and probabilistic interpolation, and do not flag when they are producing uncertain information. The generic warnings present on some platforms do not identify the individual false piece of data: they merely advise that verification is recommended.

Conclusion

AI hallucinations are not a problem the next update will solve. They are a structural feature of how these systems work, and in a professional context the cost of an unverified error is proportional to the value of the signature that appears at the foot of the document. Lawyers, chartered accountants and labour consultants have already seen — in the American cases and now in the Philadelphia incident — where blind delegation to AI leads.

The answer is not to stop using AI: it is to build around it a process that keeps the model's output clearly separate from the professional's liability. Classify the tasks, define the mandatory checks, document who reviewed what. That is not bureaucracy: it is the difference between a tool that works for you and one that signs in your place.
