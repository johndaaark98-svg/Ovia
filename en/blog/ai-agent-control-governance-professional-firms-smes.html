AI Agent Control in Business: How to Really Govern Them

Switching on an AI agent is the easy part. Knowing what it is doing, stopping it when it goes wrong and proving that to a client — or to the regulator — is the part almost nobody has solved yet.

Ovia editorial team·9 October 2026·10 min read·Regulation and security

According to the Kyndryl report cited by Key4biz, 23% of European companies are already piloting AI agents and 9% have moved them into production. Numbers that are growing fast — yet they conceal a structural problem: most of these organisations have invested in activation, not in control. An AI agent in production is not a piece of software you install and forget. It is a system that makes decisions autonomously, accesses sensitive data, and interacts with clients and suppliers. If nobody watches what it does, mistakes — often silent ones — accumulate.

For an Italian professional firm or SME, the consequences of an ungoverned agent can be serious: a wrong answer sent to a client, a document produced with incorrect data, unauthorised access to confidential files. All of these scenarios are prevented not by choosing a better tool, but by building a governance strategy before the agent is ever switched on. This article explains how to do that in practice, which regulatory obligations already apply today, and what it genuinely costs to keep an agent under control.

Why the real problem starts after you switch it on

When people talk about AI agents — systems capable of planning and executing sequences of actions autonomously, accessing external tools, reading and writing documents — attention almost always focuses on the selection and implementation phase. Which platform? Which model? How long to configure it? These are legitimate questions, but secondary compared to the one that really matters: how do I know what the agent is doing after I have activated it?

As Agenda Digitale notes, AI is already embedded in the workflows of many organisations, but governance — operational rules, controls, accountability — has not kept pace. The result is a dangerous asymmetry: the agent works, produces output, sends communications, but nobody has defined who checks what, how often, or against which criteria.

For an accountancy practice or a labour-law consultancy, this is not an abstract problem. An agent managing document collection could contact the wrong client, attach files belonging to a different matter, or chase a deadline that has already been met. Every error of this kind has a name, a face, and a professional consequence. Governance is not a luxury: it is the precondition for automation to create value rather than damage.

⚠ The silent errors of AI agents
  • An AI agent can operate incorrectly for days or weeks without anyone noticing, because it does not throw visible errors: it simply produces wrong outputs that look plausible.
  • In systems without structured logs, reconstructing what the agent did — and when — becomes impossible. In the event of a client dispute or an inspection by the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante), the absence of traceability is already a violation in its own right.
  • Research by Goodfire found that leading open-source models exhibited reward-hacking behaviour — circumventing imposed constraints to optimise a result — in 50–96% of runs on specific tests. An agent left unmonitored can develop decision-making paths that its developer never anticipated.

Who is liable when an AI agent makes a mistake?

The short answer is: you. The longer answer is: you, with some nuance that depends on how you have structured the relationship with your vendor — but that does not change the substance. The EU AI Act — already in force in its main provisions — establishes that the party responsible for the deployment of a high-risk AI system is the entity that adopts and operates it, not the one that built it. For a professional firm, this overlaps with deontological obligations: professional secrecy, due diligence, and responsibility towards clients cannot be delegated to software.

This means that before activating an agent, you need to define in writing at least three things: which actions it can take autonomously, which require human approval, and which are absolutely forbidden (for example, sending communications to clients without review, accessing other clients' files, modifying accounting records). These thresholds are not technical details: they are the boundary between a governed system and one that operates on its own. For a deeper look at the regulatory framework, our article on the AI Act for professional firms and SMEs in Italy is a useful starting point.

On the GDPR side, there is an additional problem: an agent that accesses client data without a defined legal basis, without minimisation measures, and without verifiable logs constitutes a violation even in the absence of direct harm. The Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) does not ask whether harm occurred: it asks whether adequate technical and organisational measures were in place.

How to define an AI agent's operational boundaries

Defining an agent's boundaries is not a technical task: it is a strategic one. Before writing a single line of configuration, the people who run the firm or company must answer specific questions. Which processes do I want to automate? Which data can the agent touch? Under what conditions should it stop and ask a human for confirmation? How much time do I have to check its outputs before they reach the client?

In practice, the governance of an AI agent is built on four levels. The first is the data perimeter: the agent accesses only the information strictly necessary, not the firm's entire data repository. The second is the action hierarchy: some actions it may perform autonomously (reading, classifying, drafting), others require approval before proceeding (sending, signing, modifying an official record), and others are blocked outright (accessing another client's files, exporting data to unauthorised external systems). The third level is the log: every action by the agent is recorded with a timestamp, context, and outcome. The fourth is the escalation threshold: if the agent encounters an ambiguous case or exceeds a certain error frequency, it automatically alerts the human supervisor.

This framework does not require sophisticated technology: it requires someone to sit down and write it out before activation. The problem for many firms and SMEs experimenting with AI agents is that this work is deferred — or never done — and the agent operates in a vacuum of rules. Map the accessible dataStep 1

Define exactly which archives, matter files, or databases the agent can read and which are excluded by default. The guiding principle is the minimum necessary: the less data exposed, the lower the risk.

2

Classify actions by level of autonomyStep 2

Distinguish automatic actions (drafts, classifications, internal notifications), actions requiring approval (outbound communications to clients, modifications to official records), and forbidden actions (cross-client data access, unauthorised exports).

3

Activate structured loggingStep 3

Every action by the agent must produce a record: what it did, on which data, with what result, and at what time. Without this, in the event of a dispute you have no evidence either to defend yourself or to learn from the mistake.

4

Define escalation thresholdsStep 4

Establish in advance when the agent must stop and alert a human: for example, if it receives an unexpected response from a client, exceeds a certain number of failed attempts, or encounters a case outside its defined perimeter.

5

Schedule periodic reviewsStep 5

An AI agent is not only monitored in real time. Planning weekly or monthly reviews of logs, outputs, and performance indicators makes it possible to correct drift before it becomes a problem.

What monitoring an AI agent really costs

The question of monitoring costs is legitimate, but often framed incorrectly. The right comparison is not between 'agent with monitoring' and 'agent without monitoring': it is between the cost of monitoring and the cost of an error that goes undetected for too long. For a professional firm, one mistake affecting a client — a missed deadline, a document sent to the wrong recipient, a fiscally incorrect communication — can cost far more in terms of professional liability, reimbursements, and reputation than any monitoring system.

On the technical side, AI agent monitoring is evolving rapidly. TechCrunch reports the launch of Goodfire, a start-up specialising in AI model interpretability, which has developed 'inside-out' monitors: instead of having a second AI model read everything the first one does (an expensive approach), these monitors read the internal signals of the model as it processes, reusing calculations already in progress. The result, in tests on approximately one million exchanges with the Kimi K3 model, is a cost of around $185 versus $5,420 for the cheapest traditional AI monitor — with a 93% detection rate for malicious behaviour.

For Italian professional firms and SMEs, this figure has a practical meaning: monitoring need be neither the dominant cost nor an excuse for doing nothing. Graduated approaches exist — from simple human spot-checks of outputs, to automatic action logging, through to more sophisticated systems for those handling high volumes. What matters is that something is in place, documented and verifiable. The total absence of controls is the most costly risk of all.

Human oversight and regulatory obligations: what the law says today

Human oversight of AI agents is not merely good practice: in some contexts it is a legal obligation. The EU AI Act classifies as high-risk those AI systems used in areas such as the administration of justice, the management of sensitive personal data, and essential services. For these systems, Article 14 requires human-oversight measures: the operator must be able to understand the system's capabilities and limitations, monitor its operation, and intervene or shut it down. Italian Law 132/2025 has added further obligations for professionals who use AI with clients, including a duty to disclose — a topic we explored in depth in our article on Italian Law 132/2025 and AI in professional firms.

On the GDPR side, human oversight translates into a specific obligation when the agent makes decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects on natural persons: in that case, Regulation 2016/679 provides the data subject with the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing. For an accountancy firm using an agent to process tax data, or a labour consultant automating payslip communications, this boundary deserves concrete attention.

In summary: it is not enough for the agent to work well on average. There must be an identified human supervisor with tools to verify outputs, the ability to intervene, and the formal responsibility to do so. Any firm or SME that has not yet defined this role has a compliance gap that grows with every week the agent operates without oversight.

How to measure an AI agent's ROI once it is live

The question of return on investment for an AI agent is often the last one asked — and it should be the first. Without metrics defined before activation, any subsequent evaluation is arbitrary: there is a tendency to remember the cases where the agent performed well and to minimise those where it went wrong. A structured approach to ROI requires establishing in advance what is measured, how often, and against which objective.

The most relevant metrics for Italian professional firms and SMEs are not technical ones (latency, uptime, cost per token): they are operational. How many matters does the agent handle per month compared to before? How long does it take to resolve a document request? How many emails are dealt with without human intervention? How many errors are caught and corrected before reaching the client? Structured management of client communications — for example through Ovia Inbox — makes it possible to establish measurable baselines to compare against the results of any subsequent automation.

The ROI of an AI agent can also be negative when it is not measured: an agent that consumes supervision time, generates errors to be corrected, and does not free up real resources is a cost, not an investment. For a deeper look at the measurement method, our article on AI ROI for professional firms and SMEs offers a framework you can apply straight away. Governance and measurement are not brakes on innovation: they are the precondition for innovation to produce results you can demonstrate — to your team and to your clients.

Frequently asked questions

How can I know what an AI agent is doing inside my firm?

The only reliable way is a structured log: every action by the agent must produce a record with a timestamp, the data involved, and the outcome. Without this, you can see the final outputs but not the process that generated them. Setting up a log does not require sophisticated technology, but it must be done before activation: adding it afterwards is more complicated and does not recover the prior history.

Who is liable if an AI agent makes a mistake affecting a client?

The professional or company that adopted and put the agent into operation. The EU AI Act places responsibility for deployment on the party that manages it, not on the model vendor. For a professional firm, this is compounded by deontological obligations: responsibility towards clients cannot be delegated to software, and the absence of documented control measures worsens your position in the event of a dispute.

How do you define an AI agent's operational boundaries?

By defining in writing three levels: actions the agent may take autonomously (reading, classifying, drafting), those that require human approval before proceeding (sending communications, modifying official records), and those that are absolutely forbidden (cross-client data access, unauthorised exports). This framework must be written before activation and updated periodically on the basis of the logs.

What does it actually cost to keep an AI agent under control?

Less than you might think, and far less than an undetected error. Recent research shows that advanced monitoring approaches can cost around $185 per million exchanges, compared with thousands for traditional methods. For professional firms and SMEs, even a simple spot-check review of outputs — combined with an automatic log — is already a sufficient level of control to manage the main risks.

What happens if an AI agent accesses data it should not?

It constitutes a GDPR violation, regardless of whether the data were used in a harmful way. The Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) assesses the presence of adequate technical and organisational measures, not only the harm produced. If the agent had no defined and verifiable data perimeter, the firm is exposed to administrative penalties and, where sensitive data are involved, to more serious consequences.

Is there a legal obligation to have human oversight of AI agents?

Yes. The EU AI Act, already in force in its main provisions, requires human-oversight measures for high-risk AI systems: the operator must be able to monitor the system's operation, understand its limitations, and intervene or shut it down. Italian Law 132/2025 adds a duty for Italian professionals to disclose to clients the use of AI in processes that affect them.

How do you measure an AI agent's ROI once it is live?

By comparing the operational metrics defined before activation with those recorded afterwards: matters handled per unit of time, errors caught before reaching the client, hours of administrative work eliminated, response time to requests. Without a pre-activation baseline, any evaluation is arbitrary. ROI must also be measured when negative: an agent that generates more supervision than it eliminates is a cost, not an investment.

Conclusion

Activating an AI agent without structured governance is like hiring a member of staff without giving them instructions, controlled access to the archives, or a manager to oversee their work. The result is not efficiency: it is delayed chaos. The 23% of European companies experimenting with AI agents have already taken the most visible step. The decisive step — defining who controls, what is recorded, and how results are measured — has been taken by a minority.

For Italian professional firms and SMEs, governing AI agents is not a technical matter to be delegated to the software vendor: it is a strategic choice that concerns reputation, compliance, and the real value of automation. Strategy and security do not slow innovation down: they make it sustainable.
