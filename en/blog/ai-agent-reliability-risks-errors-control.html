AI Agent Reliability: Risks, Errors and Control

Real-world benchmarks record high failure rates even on the most advanced models. For a professional firm or Italian SME, delegating operations to an AI agent without documented governance means exposure to three concrete risks: undetected errors, direct liability, and loss of control over sensitive data.

Ovia editorial team·25 November 2026·8 min read·Regulation and security

Imagine delegating a significant share of tasks to a member of staff, knowing in advance that some of them will be carried out incorrectly. No firm owner or SME director would accept those terms. Yet that is precisely the situation organisations face when they adopt AI agents without documented governance: the most recent benchmarks — including the ShellBench study — record failure rates approaching nearly four in ten tasks, even on models considered the most advanced.

The problem is not technological: it is strategic. The right question is not 'which agent should we use?' but 'which tasks can we genuinely delegate, with what verification, and with what traceability?' For a professional firm — accountants, lawyers, employment consultants — or for an Italian SME, the wrong answer to that question does not merely produce inefficiency: it produces direct liability towards clients, regulatory authorities, and in some cases before a criminal court.

How often does an AI agent actually fail? The numbers that matter

The most honest answer comes from benchmarks designed to measure AI agent performance on concrete tasks, not abstract questions. The ShellBench study, cited by AI4Business, puts models through real system operations — file management, script execution, environment navigation — and records failure rates approaching nearly four in ten tasks, even on the best models. We are not talking about marginal errors on edge cases: a significant proportion of trials simply do not reach the expected result.

For a professional firm, this figure has an immediate practical meaning. If an AI agent handles client document collection, email categorisation, or draft preparation, one in every three or four operations could be wrong — and the trouble is that the error often produces no visible error message: the agent completes the workflow, but partially or incorrectly. The silent error is the most dangerous, because it triggers no control.

This does not mean AI agents are useless. It means that the level of autonomy granted to them must be calibrated against the criticality of the task and the reversibility of any error. An agent that sorts emails can mis-categorise a message: it can be corrected. An agent that sends communications to clients or accesses confidential tax data without supervision can cause damage that is far harder to undo.

Who is liable when an AI agent makes a mistake? Real consequences for firms and SMEs

Liability is the most underestimated issue in AI agent adoption. When a professional firm or SME integrates an AI agent into its processes — even using a third-party platform — it takes on the role of deployer under the European AI Act. This means it is responsible for the system's concrete use: the instructions given to the agent, the tasks delegated, and the controls activated or not activated.

On the Italian Legislative Decree 231/2001 front, the reasoning extends further. Decree 231 establishes the administrative liability of an organisation for offences committed in its interest or to its advantage. If an AI agent makes decisions — even partial ones — in areas such as contract management, client communications, or the submission of tax documents, and no documented control framework is in place, any irregularities risk falling on the organisation. The argument 'I didn't know, the AI did it' provides no legal defence.

NIS2 adds a third layer: organisations within scope must ensure the security of automated systems that process data or operate on critical infrastructure. An AI agent that accesses emails, client databases, or management systems is, in every practical sense, a component of the organisation's IT infrastructure and falls within cyber risk management obligations. You can explore the topic further in our article on AI liability for professional firms and SMEs in 2026.

Important: Adopting a third-party AI agent does not transfer liability to the supplier. The deployer is responsible for its use, the instructions given, and the absence of controls. The AI Act, Italian Legislative Decree 231, and NIS2 all converge on this point.

How an AI agent accesses your data: the risk you cannot see

An AI agent does not 'read' data the way a human does: it traverses it systematically, often without the firm owner knowing exactly which files, emails, or records were consulted during a session. AI agent security is already a live issue that has led organisations such as Signal and several banks to block or restrict the use of AI agents on their systems, precisely because of the impossibility of controlling the actual breadth of access.

For a professional firm this is a direct professional-secrecy problem. If an AI agent has access to an entire email inbox or document archive, it may — while executing a specific task — pass over data belonging to other clients, confidential communications, or ongoing matters. The risk is not theoretical: it is structural in the way most AI agents are implemented today, with broad permissions and access logs that are absent or unreadable.

The solution is not to abandon AI agents: it is to design the system on the principle of least privilege — granting the agent only the access strictly necessary for the task at hand, with verifiable logs and periodic permission reviews. This requires a design phase: it is not a five-minute configuration on a tool's dashboard.

What the AI Act requires for AI agent control within organisations

The AI Act does not prohibit AI agents: it regulates them. For uses in professional contexts — which often fall into medium- or high-risk categories — the European regulation places precise obligations on the deployer: a risk assessment before adoption, technical and organisational measures to ensure human supervision, and documentation of decisions made with or through the AI system. You can find a detailed breakdown of the 2026 obligations in our article on AI Act obligations: DPIA, LIA and FRIA.

Human supervision does not mean 'a person watching the screen while the agent works'. It means a defined and documented process: who reviews the agent's outputs, how often, according to what criteria, and what happens when an error is detected. This process must exist before the agent goes live — not after the first incident.

For professional firms there is an additional layer: Italian Law 132/2025 introduced the obligation to inform clients when AI tools are used in professional services. If an AI agent contributes — even automatically — to producing a document or managing a matter, the client has the right to know. The disclosure is not a mere formality: it is a condition for the validity of the professional relationship.

The right strategy: decide what to delegate before choosing a tool

Control of the entire system is the point on which all agentic AI experts agree: it is not enough for the model to 'decide well' in isolation. The agent operates in a context — real data, real systems, real consequences — and control must be designed across the entire workflow, not just on the final output.

For a firm or SME, the right strategy unfolds in three steps. First: map the tasks that are candidates for delegation and classify them by criticality (reversibility of errors, impact on sensitive data, involvement of third parties). Second: define for each task the permissible level of autonomy and the mandatory human checkpoint. Third: build the traceability system before the agent goes live, not as an afterthought.

This approach does not slow adoption: it accelerates it sustainably. A firm that knows exactly what it has delegated, with what safeguards and with what documentation, can progressively extend the agent's autonomy as it accumulates real reliability data from its own operational context. Those who adopt an agent without governance end up managing incidents rather than preventing them — and in a professional context, an incident almost always costs more than whatever efficiency was gained. See also our article on the limits of AI agents in business for a deeper look at the forms of human judgement that cannot be delegated.

1

Map and classify tasksPhase 1 — Before choosing a tool

List the tasks that are candidates for delegation. For each one, assess: error criticality, reversibility, involvement of sensitive data or third parties. Exclude from automatic delegation anything that is irreversible or that touches professional secrecy without supervision.

2

Define autonomy levels and checkpointsPhase 2 — Before going live

For each delegated task, establish the agent's maximum level of autonomy and the point at which a human must approve or verify. Document these decisions: they form the foundation of your AI Act register and your Decree 231 compliance framework.

3

Build logs and permission restrictionsPhase 3 — Technical configuration

Implement the principle of least privilege: the agent accesses only the data needed for the specific task. Enable readable action logs. Appoint an internal person responsible for reviewing them on a defined schedule.

4

Test, measure and adjustPhase 4 — Ongoing operations

Do not rely on generic benchmarks: measure your agent's error rate in your own real operational context. Use this data to decide whether and where to extend autonomy. Trust is earned through data, not vendor promises.

Frequently asked questions

Are AI agents truly reliable for my firm or SME?

It depends on the task and the governance framework. ShellBench benchmarks record failure rates approaching nearly four in ten tasks, even on the most advanced models, in real-world scenarios. AI agents can be reliable for repetitive, low-risk tasks, provided a documented supervision system exists and permissions are limited to what is necessary. Without these conditions, operational reliability cannot be guaranteed.

How often does an AI agent fail on real tasks?

According to the ShellBench study, the best AI models fail nearly four in ten trials on real system tasks. The figure is significant because many errors produce no visible alert: the agent completes the workflow, but partially or incorrectly. This is precisely why human supervision is not optional.

Who is liable if an AI agent makes an error?

The person or organisation that adopts and manages the agent — the deployer — is responsible for its use under the AI Act, regardless of whether the system belongs to a third-party supplier. Italian Legislative Decree 231/2001 can extend liability to the organisation if the agent's decisions have produced irregularities in the organisation's interest, in the absence of a documented control framework. The argument 'I didn't know, the AI decided' is not a recognised legal defence.

How do I control what an AI agent can do and see on my data?

Through the principle of least privilege: the agent must have access only to the data and systems strictly necessary for the specific task. You need to enable readable action logs, appoint an internal person responsible for reviewing them periodically, and revise permissions whenever the agent's operational scope changes. This configuration must be designed before the agent goes live.

What does the AI Act require for AI agent control within organisations?

The AI Act requires the deployer to carry out a risk assessment before adoption, implement technical and organisational measures to ensure human supervision, and document decisions made using the AI system. For medium- to high-risk systems — a category that covers many professional uses — these obligations are binding and verifiable at audit.

Does Italian Legislative Decree 231/2001 apply to decisions made by AI?

Yes, to the extent that the AI agent's decisions produce legally relevant effects in the interest or to the advantage of the organisation. If no documented control framework is in place — a process that defines who reviews the agent's actions, how often, and according to what criteria — there is a risk that any irregularities will fall under the organisation's liability pursuant to Decree 231.

How do I record and document AI agent actions to stay compliant?

The minimum required is: readable action logs from the agent, an up-to-date permissions register, a human supervision process with a named person responsible and a defined frequency, and documentation of the initial risk assessment. For professional firms, the client disclosure required by Italian Law 132/2025 is also mandatory. These elements form the documentary basis for AI Act, NIS2, and Decree 231 compliance.

Conclusion

Delegating to an AI agent without documented governance is not a technology choice: it is a risk choice. Real-world benchmarks record failure rates approaching nearly four in ten tasks; the AI Act, Italian Legislative Decree 231, and NIS2 all attribute liability to the deployer; and the loss of control over access to sensitive data is structural, not hypothetical. For a professional firm or Italian SME, the question is not 'whether to adopt AI agents' but 'which tasks to delegate, with what supervision, and with what traceability'.

Strategy comes before the tool. Mapping processes, restricting permissions, building logs, and defining mandatory human checkpoints are not activities that slow innovation: they are the condition under which innovation delivers real results instead of risk exposure.
