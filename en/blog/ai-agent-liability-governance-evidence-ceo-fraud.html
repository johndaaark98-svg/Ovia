AI Agent Liability: Who Is Responsible When Things Go Wrong?

An AI agent can send emails, initiate bank transfers and access documents without you pressing a single key. But if something goes wrong, the liability is still yours. Here is what you need to know — and document — before that happens.

Ovia editorial team·27 September 2026·10 min read·Regulation and security

An AI agent is not software that waits for your commands: it is a system that decides, acts and completes tasks autonomously. It sends an email to a client, updates a deadline, drafts a contract, sorts documents — all in sequence, without asking for your approval at every step. That is precisely what makes it useful. And it is precisely why AI agent liability has become an urgent issue for businesses and professional firms.

Until recently, the legal debate around artificial intelligence focused on output: a wrong answer, an inaccurate forecast, a flawed document. The human received the result and decided what to do with it. With AI agents, the breaking point shifts: the system does not merely produce an output — it takes an action. And an action can have legal, financial and reputational consequences before you are even aware of it.

Three elements make this especially concrete for anyone running a professional firm or an SME in Italy: real-world cases of CEO fraud supercharged by voice deepfakes — such as the €95 million fraud targeting Fideuram —, the low awareness of AI scams among Italians confirmed by the National Privacy Test 2026, and the new traceability requirements introduced by Italian Law 132/2025. Ignoring them is not an option.

This article answers the questions every firm owner or business manager should ask before deploying an AI agent: who is liable when something goes wrong, what needs to be documented, how to defend against CEO fraud, and how to build a control framework that holds up even in litigation.

Who is liable if an AI agent causes harm?

The short answer is: you. Or rather, the firm or professional who chose to deploy the agent, defined its permissions and embedded it in their processes. As Agenda Digitale explains, the shift from outputs to actions moves the centre of gravity of liability: it is no longer a matter of assessing whether a piece of text was correct, but of establishing whether an action was authorised, proportionate and traceable.

Under current Italian law — and even more so under the framework of the EU AI Act — high-risk AI systems require human oversight. But 'oversight' does not mean watching from the sidelines: it means having written procedures, action logs, explicit limits and a named responsible person. If the agent acts beyond the boundaries you set, the judge's question will be: how do you demonstrate that those limits existed and were being enforced?

For a professional firm this question carries additional weight: an accountant, lawyer or employment consultant is also answerable on disciplinary grounds. An AI agent that sends a confidential document to the wrong client is not merely a technical error — it may constitute a breach of professional secrecy with disciplinary consequences. Agent governance is therefore, in every respect, part of the governance of the firm itself.

CEO fraud and voice deepfakes: the risk nobody sees coming

In February 2026, Fideuram fell victim to a fraud totalling approximately €95 million, with €36 million still unrecovered. The fraudsters used WhatsApp messages that appeared to come from Intesa Sanpaolo CEO Carlo Messina, combined with an AI-cloned voice imitating that of a well-known business lawyer. The combination of urgency, perceived authority and a familiar voice persuaded the right people to authorise the transfers.

This is not an isolated case. As Key4biz reports, AI-powered CEO fraud has already struck Massimo Moratti (nearly €1 million, later recovered), Maire Tecnimont (€18 million transferred to Chinese banks), Sisal (€5.5 million), and came close to hitting Ferrari and advertising group WPP. In the Arup case in Hong Kong, an employee joined a video call featuring real-time deepfake avatars of colleagues and the CFO, and transferred $25 million as a result.

The connection to AI agents is direct: if you have an agent managing emails, authorising payments or accessing business systems, a fraudulent instruction — delivered by voice, text or WhatsApp — can become the trigger for an automated action. The agent has no critical judgement about the intentions behind a command: it simply executes. This means that defending against CEO fraud can no longer rely on recognising a voice or trusting a sender; it must become procedural and independent of the AI itself.

What Italian Law 132/2025 changes for traceability and evidence

Italian Law 132/2025 introduces traceability requirements that directly affect anyone using AI agents in a professional or business context. The underlying principle is that an automated system that produces legal effects must leave verifiable traces: who gave the instruction, at what moment, with which parameters, and what action followed. Without this documentary chain, it is impossible in any dispute to separate the agent's legitimate action from an error or fraud.

For professional firms, this intersects with existing obligations — document retention, the duty to inform clients about the use of AI (already required by professional codes of conduct and reinforced by Italian Law 132/2025, as we examined in our article on Italian Law 132/2025 and client disclosure) — and GDPR compliance. An AI agent activity log is not just good practice: it is becoming an implicit compliance requirement.

The practical consequence is that anyone who deploys an AI agent today without a structured logging and evidence-retention system faces a double risk: the risk of the incident itself, and the risk of being unable to mount a defence even when the incident is not their fault. Preventive documentation is the first line of defence — not a bureaucratic box-ticking exercise to be done after the event.

How to build a control framework that holds up in litigation

A control framework for AI agents is not a technical firewall: it is a set of rules, procedures and documentary records that allow you to answer the question a judge, the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) or a client will ask: 'How do you demonstrate that the agent acted within the limits you had set?' The answer must exist before the incident — not be constructed after it.

The starting point is the principle of least privilege: the agent receives only the permissions strictly necessary for the task it is performing. If it manages incoming emails, it does not need access to the accounting system. If it processes tax documents, it does not need to send communications to clients on its own initiative. Every unnecessary permission is an additional attack surface.

The second element is the separation between the AI channel and the verification channel. CEO fraud works because it exploits urgency and trust in the channel: a familiar voice, a message that looks authentic. The defence is not technological but procedural: for any action that exceeds a defined threshold — financial, confidentiality-related or impact-related — confirmation must arrive through a different, independent channel, using a passphrase or code agreed in advance. The Ferrari case is instructive: the manager who asked the fake Vigna to name a book title stopped the fraud with a simple question.

The third element is periodic review. Agent logs must be read, not merely retained. A monthly review of anomalous actions — out-of-hours instructions, unusual recipients, unexpected data volumes — is the most effective way to catch a problem before it becomes irreversible. For anyone managing an operational CRM with automated workflows, this kind of review can be integrated directly into system reports.

1

Define the scopeStep 1

Write a policy describing what the agent can do, on which data, towards which parties, and with what quantitative or qualitative limits. This is the document you will present in the event of a challenge.

2

Apply least privilegeStep 2

Configure the agent with only the access it needs. Revisit permissions whenever its scope of action changes. Document every change with a date and reason.

3

Separate channels for critical actionsStep 3

Set a threshold beyond which the agent does not act alone: human confirmation on a separate channel is required. For financial or confidential communications, use out-of-band verification codes agreed in advance.

4

Retain and review the logsStep 4

Agent logs are evidence. Retain them for the period required by law, make them readable, and assign someone the task of reviewing them periodically.

The concrete risks for an SME or firm with no AI governance

The temptation to deploy an AI agent without first structuring governance is understandable: the tools are accessible, results are immediate, and procedures feel like a slowdown. But the data from the NordVPN National Privacy Test 2026 paint a worrying picture: only 43% of Italians recognise an AI-generated scam and just 25% can identify a deepfake. This means that the majority of employees, collaborators and even firm owners are unable to recognise a fraudulent instruction that looks authentic.

For an SME or professional firm, the concrete risks operate at three levels. The first is operational risk: a poorly configured agent that sends the wrong document to the wrong client, executes an out-of-context action, or gets stuck in a loop on a critical deadline. The second is fraud risk: a fraudulent instruction — delivered by voice, text or email — that the agent carries out because it falls within its defined scope and it has no means to question it. The third is legal and reputational risk: the inability to demonstrate, after the incident, that the agent had defined limits and that those limits were being enforced.

Anyone who has already read our article on AI agent risks in professional firms knows that the problem is not AI itself, but the way in which it is integrated into processes. A well-governed AI agent reduces operational risk compared with manual processes, because it is traceable and consistent. An AI agent without governance amplifies it, because it acts autonomously on foundations that nobody has ever committed to writing.

⚠ Warning: these signs indicate insufficient AI governance
  • The agent has access to email inboxes, business systems or document archives without a written policy delimiting its use.
  • There is no retained log of the agent's actions — or one exists but nobody reads it.
  • Instructions to the agent can arrive via voice, WhatsApp or email without an independent verification procedure for high-risk actions.
  • No employee or collaborator has received specific training on how to recognise an AI scam or a voice deepfake.
  • The client disclosure notice has not been updated to include the use of automated systems in the delivery of services.

Why email and communications management is the first control point

If there is one area where AI agent governance has an immediate impact for a professional firm or SME, it is inbound communications management. Emails, certified electronic mail (PEC), messages: these are the primary vector for CEO fraud, the channel through which fraudulent instructions arrive, and often the first system to be automated with an AI agent.

An agent managing an email inbox must have clear rules about what it can do on its own and what it must bring to a human's attention before acting. A draft response prepared by the agent and approved by the principal is a sound governance model: the efficiency of automation combined with human control over sensitive content. This is very different from an agent that sends independently, with nobody seeing what has gone out and to whom.

Equally, call transcription and summarisation — with client attribution and structured retention — automatically builds part of the audit trail you need for compliance: who asked what, when, and what response was given. This is not just operational efficiency: it is documentary traceability that counts in litigation too.

Frequently asked questions

Who is liable if an AI agent makes a mistake or causes harm?

Liability falls on the firm or professional who deployed and configured the AI agent, not on the software provider. Under the EU AI Act and current Italian law, anyone deploying an automated system in a professional or business context must ensure human oversight, defined limits and traceability of actions. If the agent acts beyond the boundaries set for it, the critical point is being able to demonstrate that those limits existed and were being enforced.

How do I prove that an AI agent acted within the limits I set?

Through preventive documentation: a written policy defining the agent's scope of action, retained logs of every action with timestamps and parameters, and a named internal responsible person. This documentation must exist before any incident — it cannot be reconstructed after the fact. Italian Law 132/2025 reinforces these requirements in terms of traceability and evidence.

What do I need to document before deploying an AI agent in my firm or company?

At minimum, four elements: a written policy covering the agent's scope and limits, a list of assigned permissions (applying the principle of least privilege), an action log system retained for the period required by law, and a named internal owner. You should also update your client disclosure to cover the use of automated systems, and put in place human verification procedures for high-risk actions.

How do I protect my firm against CEO fraud and voice deepfakes when using AI in communications?

The defence is procedural, not technological. For any action exceeding a defined threshold — payments, sending confidential documents, changes to sensitive data — confirmation must come through a different channel, independent of the AI system, using a passphrase or code agreed in advance. Voice is no longer a reliable authentication method: the Fideuram case (€95M attempt) and the Ferrari case make this abundantly clear.

Do the new Italian rules change anything about AI agent liability?

Yes. Italian Law 132/2025 introduces traceability requirements that make preventive documentation not just good practice but a necessity. Anyone using AI agents in a professional context must be able to reconstruct the chain of instructions and actions in the event of a dispute. This is in addition to existing obligations under GDPR, professional secrecy rules and client disclosure requirements.

What are the concrete risks for an SME or firm that deploys AI agents without governance?

Three levels of risk: operational (wrong actions, data sent to the wrong recipients, missed deadlines), fraud (fraudulent instructions executed by the agent because they fall within its defined scope), and legal-reputational (inability to demonstrate that the agent had defined limits in the event of a challenge). The National Privacy Test 2026 confirms that only 25% of Italians can identify a deepfake, which amplifies the fraud risk considerably.

How do you build an AI agent control framework that is defensible in litigation?

Four essential elements: a written scope of action, minimum necessary permissions, logs that are retained and reviewed periodically, and separation between the AI channel and the verification channel for critical actions. Periodic log review is crucial: retaining logs is not enough — they must be read in order to spot anomalies before they become incidents.

Conclusion

AI agents are powerful tools and, when used well, they genuinely transform the way a firm or business operates. But 'used well' means with a defined scope, retained logs, verification procedures for critical actions and a human maintaining oversight. This is not an obstacle to innovation: it is the condition that makes innovation sustainable.

The CEO fraud cases — from Fideuram to Maire Tecnimont, from Moratti to Arup — demonstrate that the problem is not AI itself, but blind trust in the channel and in perceived authority. The same trust that, left ungoverned, can turn a useful AI agent into an executor of fraudulent instructions. Voice is no longer enough. The sender's identity is no longer enough. Procedures are essential.

If you are considering introducing AI agents into your firm or business, the first step is not choosing the tool: it is defining what it can do, what it cannot do, and how you will demonstrate it. Ovia can help you build this framework before activating any automation.
