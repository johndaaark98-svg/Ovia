AI Cyberattacks: What Firms and SMEs Risk Today

Cybercriminals are already using AI to attack professional firms and SMEs in Italy. New European regulations are beginning to impose concrete obligations: here is what is changing and what to do right now.

Ovia editorial team·20 November 2026·9 min read·Regulation and security

Any professional who thinks 'cybercriminals go after banks and multinationals, not me' is making the most expensive mistake of 2026. AI-powered cyberattacks have driven down the cost of targeting a small organisation: today a criminal group can launch thousands of personalised campaigns in parallel, each tailored to deceive a single firm or small business — at virtually zero marginal cost to the attacker.

Meanwhile, three European regulations — NIS2, DORA and the Cyber Resilience Act — are redrawing the boundaries of legal responsibility for security. These are not rules reserved for large corporations: their chain of application reaches down to digital service providers and, indirectly, to those who use them. Understanding what they require, and building a concrete response today, is the difference between a resilient firm and an exposed one.

ⓘ Key takeaways
  • AI lowers the cost of attacks and makes them almost impossible to spot with the naked eye: phishing, BEC and voice fraud are now personalised and grammatically flawless.
  • NIS2 extends security obligations to the digital service providers used by firms and SMEs; DORA directly affects anyone working with the financial sector; the Cyber Resilience Act sets standards for software manufacturers and connected device makers.
  • Dependence on a single cloud provider — as recent Microsoft outage warnings have demonstrated — is a concrete operational risk, not a theoretical one.
  • The obligation to report a serious cyber incident is now measured in hours, not days: without a ready-made procedure, the deadline expires before you have even worked out what happened.
  • The first line of defence is not technical: it is the ability to recognise an attack attempt before it becomes an incident, by keeping a structured watch over incoming emails and documents.
  • Before adopting any AI tool in your firm, you must map the real operational risks and build continuity that does not depend on a single point of failure.

How AI is transforming cyberattacks against professional firms and SMEs

Until a few years ago, a phishing attempt was easy to spot: clumsy Italian, a suspicious sender, a generic tone that never matched the context. Those tells have disappeared. Criminal groups now use language models to generate emails that replicate a colleague's writing style, reference a real case file, use the client's name and arrive at exactly the right moment. As documented by Cybersecurity360, traditional behaviour-based detection systems are no longer enough: approaches that analyse the intent of a message, not just its form, are now required.

For an accountancy practice or a labour-law consultant, the practical consequences are immediate. An urgent wire-transfer request that appears to come from the firm's principal, an email from a client asking to change the IBAN for an invoice payment, a tax-deadline notification with an embedded link — each of these scenarios can now be replicated by AI in seconds, at industrial scale. The article on AI email fraud and BEC attacks covers the most common techniques and how to recognise them in detail.

The critical issue goes beyond phishing alone. AI accelerates every phase of an attack: reconnaissance (gathering information about the firm from public websites, social media and registers), intrusion (exploiting unpatched vulnerabilities) and persistence (remaining inside systems undetected). Anyone without an active monitoring procedure has no way of knowing whether they have already been compromised.

NIS2, DORA and the Cyber Resilience Act: who is obligated and what must they do

The most common question is: 'Do these rules really apply to my firm or small business?' The honest answer is: it depends — but more than you might think. As Agenda Digitale explains, the new European regulations follow a value-chain logic: even if your firm is not directly subject to NIS2, the providers of your practice-management software, your cloud services and your e-invoicing platforms are. If one of those providers suffers a serious incident, you need a contingency plan and, in some cases, you must be able to demonstrate that you carried out appropriate security checks on them.

NIS2 (transposed into Italian law by Legislative Decree 138/2024) applies directly to essential and important entities in critical sectors, but its reach extends to the digital service providers used by those entities. If one of your clients is a company subject to NIS2, they can ask you for assurances about your own security chain. DORA (the Digital Operational Resilience Act) covers the financial sector and its ICT suppliers: if you provide services to banks, insurers or intermediaries, you are in scope. The Cyber Resilience Act imposes security standards on manufacturers of software and connected devices: anyone selling digital tools must guarantee timely patches throughout the entire commercial life of the product.

On the notification obligations front, the timescales are tight. NIS2 requires a preliminary notification to the competent authority within 24 hours of discovering a significant incident, and a full notification within 72 hours. DORA operates on similar timelines. Without a procedure already in place — an incident log, a designated contact, the authority's details — these deadlines expire before the crisis meeting is even over.

The single-vendor risk: the cloud outage case

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella has publicly stated that as AI complexity increases, outages could multiply: ever more interconnected systems widen the attack surface and raise the probability of cascading failures. As AI4Business reports, the warning covers not only intentional attacks but also technical errors amplified by interdependence between services.

For a professional firm that has concentrated its email, documents, video calls and practice management on Microsoft 365, even a partial outage shuts everything down simultaneously. The issue is already explored in depth in the article on Microsoft 365 outages and cloud dependency risks, but the regulatory perspective adds a new dimension: NIS2 explicitly requires entities in scope to have documented and tested business continuity measures. Relying entirely on a single provider with no fallback plan is not merely an operational risk — it can become a compliance failure.

The solution is not necessarily to abandon the cloud, but to avoid concentrating all critical services in a single point of failure. That means regularly verified offline backups, a manual procedure for the most urgent deadlines, and — for communications at least — an alternative channel that can be activated within a few hours. As the analysis of US outages as a case study shows, the organisations that recovered fastest were not those with the most sophisticated systems, but those with the simplest, already-tested emergency procedures.

Warning: If you use the same provider for email, documents, video calls and practice management, a single incident — technical or cyber — shuts down the entire firm. NIS2 explicitly requires documented business continuity measures: not having them can become a legal liability on top of an operational loss.

How to tell whether your emails or documents have already been compromised

One of the most insidious scenarios is silent compromise: an attacker gains access to your systems, monitors communications for weeks and only strikes at the most profitable moment — for instance by redirecting a payment or impersonating the principal during a negotiation. The problem is that, without active monitoring tools, this type of intrusion produces no obvious signals.

Warning signs to watch for are often subtle: emails showing as read before you open them, clients replying to messages you have no recollection of sending, logins to your practice management system at unusual hours, documents modified without anyone in the firm having done so. None of these signals is conclusive proof in itself, but each warrants an immediate check with your IT contact or service provider.

On the preventive side, structured management of incoming emails makes a tangible difference: knowing who opened what and when, and spotting anomalies against normal patterns, is the first layer of defence. A system such as Ovia Inbox works at exactly this layer — identifying every incoming communication by client, subject and urgency — and surfaces anomalies that would otherwise remain invisible in the daily flow. Equally, structured oversight of incoming documents reduces the risk of a malicious file being opened without verification.

Reset the passwords for critical accounts (email, practice management, cloud) using a different device from the suspect one. Enable two-factor authentication immediately if it is not already active.

3

Contact a security professionalStep 3

A real cyber incident requires forensic expertise: do not attempt to clean the system yourself. Document everything — logs, screenshots, suspicious emails — before any intervention.

4

Assess notification obligationsStep 4

If personal data is involved, you have 72 hours to notify the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) under the GDPR. If the organisation falls under NIS2 or DORA, the notification deadlines to the sectoral authority are even tighter: 24 hours for the preliminary report.

5

Inform the clients affectedStep 5

If the compromise involved client data, prompt communication — as well as being a legal obligation — is a measure of protection for the professional relationship. Prepare a clear, measured message.

The Ovia approach: map risks before adopting AI tools

There is a paradox worth naming explicitly: many firms and SMEs are evaluating AI tools to gain efficiency, yet they are doing so without first checking the solidity of the infrastructure on which those tools will run. Adding AI on top of an unmonitored digital base widens the attack surface — it does not reduce it.

Ovia's position is clear: strategy and security create value; AI is the instrument. Before automating any process — from email to document management — it is worth answering three operational questions. First: what are the most critical data for my firm's continuity? Second: who can access them today, and with what controls? Third: if my main cloud provider goes offline tomorrow morning, what happens to this week's deadlines?

This mapping exercise does not require a large-company budget. It requires method. Notification and patch obligations: the timescales you cannot ignore

One of the most practical consequences of the new European rules concerns mandatory response timescales. NIS2 requires entities directly in scope to notify a significant incident to the competent authority — in Italy, the ACN (Agenzia per la Cybersicurezza Nazionale, the National Cybersecurity Agency) — within 24 hours of discovery, with a full notification within 72 hours. The GDPR already required 72 hours for personal data breaches to the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante): NIS2 tightens the window even further for the first report.

On the security patch front, the Cyber Resilience Act introduces an obligation for software manufacturers to release critical updates 'without undue delay' throughout the entire commercial life of the product. For users of that software, this translates into a practical obligation to apply patches within a reasonable time of release: ignoring a known critical update can become a contributory factor in liability if a subsequent incident occurs.

For firms and SMEs not directly subject to NIS2, these timescales remain a useful benchmark. If you handle clients' personal data — and every professional firm does — the GDPR's 72-hour deadline already applies today. Building an incident-response procedure that meets these timescales is not red tape: it is the difference between managing an incident in a controlled manner and scrambling in a panic.

Frequently asked questions

Do AI-powered cyberattacks really concern my firm or SME too?

Yes. AI has driven down the cost for an attacker of targeting a small organisation: a criminal group can now generate thousands of personalised phishing emails in parallel, each built around the specific style and context of a particular firm. Professional practices are attractive targets because they hold sensitive data for many clients and often have limited IT oversight.

Do NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act apply directly to my firm?

NIS2 applies directly to essential and important entities in the critical sectors listed in the Italian implementing decree. Many firms do not fall within scope directly, but can be drawn in indirectly: if you provide services to a company subject to NIS2 or work with the financial sector (where DORA applies), your client can ask you for assurances about your own security chain. The Cyber Resilience Act covers software manufacturers, but it also obliges users to apply the patches released.

How can I tell whether my emails or documents have already been compromised?

Typical signs are subtle: emails showing as read before you open them, logins to your practice management system at unusual hours, documents modified without anyone in the firm having done so, or clients replying to messages you have no recollection of sending. Without a structured monitoring system, these signals go unnoticed. If you suspect a compromise, isolate the device, change credentials from a clean device and contact a security professional immediately.

What should I do if my cloud provider goes offline or is attacked?

You need a business continuity plan already in place: a copy of critical data outside the main cloud chain, a manual procedure for the most urgent deadlines and an alternative communications channel that can be activated within a few hours. NIS2 explicitly requires entities in scope to have these measures documented and tested. Even those not directly within scope should have at least an offline backup that is verified periodically.

What are the notification obligations following a cyber incident?

If you handle clients' personal data, the GDPR requires notification to the Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) within 72 hours of discovering a breach. For entities directly subject to NIS2, the preliminary notification to the ACN must be made within 24 hours, with a full notification within 72 hours. Without a procedure already in place — an incident log, a designated contact, the authority's details — these deadlines expire before the crisis meeting is even over.

How can I defend against AI-powered phishing and email fraud?

There are three fundamental countermeasures: structured oversight of the email inbox (not relying on the human eye alone to spot anomalies), out-of-band verification for any request involving a payment or change of bank details (a phone call to a known number, not a reply to the same email thread), and regular updates for all staff on the latest attack techniques. Traditional filtering systems are no longer sufficient against messages crafted by AI with no grammatical errors.

How quickly must I apply a critical security patch under the new EU rules?

The Cyber Resilience Act explicitly requires manufacturers to release critical patches 'without undue delay'. For users of the software, the practical benchmark is to apply critical updates as quickly as possible after release: ignoring a known patch can become a contributory factor in liability if a subsequent incident occurs, both under the GDPR and under NIS2.

Conclusion

AI has shifted the balance of power between attackers and defenders: by lowering the cost of attacks and raising their quality, it has made every professional firm and Italian SME an economically attractive target. The new European regulations — NIS2, DORA and the Cyber Resilience Act — respond to this transformation with concrete obligations covering liability, notification and business continuity.

The good news is that effective defence does not require a large-company budget. It requires method: mapping the real risks, monitoring incoming communications channels, avoiding concentration of everything in a single provider, and having an incident-response procedure ready before it is ever needed.

Before adding any AI tool to your firm, take a moment to verify that the foundations are sound. Strategy and security create value. AI is the instrument — but only when there is a solid base to build on.
