AI Agents for SMEs and Firms: 46% Intent, 4% Results

The Italian AI agent market is on the verge of a boom. Yet the data show that without a clear strategy, measured baselines and the right skills, most investments will produce no tangible results.

Ovia editorial team·21 November 2026·9 min read·Automation

Nearly half of Italian businesses say they intend to invest in AI agents by 2027. That is one of the most widely stated intentions in the market today — and, for anyone running a professional firm or an SME, one of the most dangerous statistics to read without context.

The danger is not AI itself. It is the enormous gap between those who *want* to adopt AI agents and those who actually get them to work in their favour. Available research shows that only 4% of Italian businesses are genuinely AI-driven, against 46% that declare the intention — a gulf of more than forty percentage points that no software subscription will close.

This article explains what creates that gap, why it hits professional firms and SMEs hardest, and where it genuinely makes sense to start in order to achieve measurable results.

What AI agents are and why they become a priority in 2027

An AI agent is an artificial intelligence system capable of executing sequences of actions autonomously, taking small decisions to reach an assigned goal: replying to an email, qualifying a contact, chasing missing documents, updating a client record. It is not simply a chatbot that answers questions — it is a system that *acts* on real processes.

Until a few years ago these systems were expensive, brittle and difficult to integrate with the software already in use at professional firms and SMEs. Today development costs have fallen significantly, and the latest generation of language models has made agents far more reliable at interpreting instructions in natural language. That is why 46% of Italian businesses have put AI agents among their investment priorities for 2027.

For an accountancy practice, an AI agent could manage the first contact with a prospective client, gather the documents needed for a filing or transcribe and summarise calls with clients. For a manufacturing SME it could follow up on outstanding quotes or update the CRM after every interaction. The potential is real. The problem is that the distance between potential and concrete results is enormous, and it almost always comes down to how the decision to invest was reached in the first place.

Why so few businesses achieve concrete results from AI

The most honest answer is that the majority of AI investments start the wrong way. The ROI of an AI project is decided — and most often lost — at the value-definition stage, before a pilot has even begun. If you do not first establish how long the process takes today, how many hours it consumes, how many errors it produces and what those errors cost in economic terms, you will never be able to prove that the AI agent improved anything.

This is a measurement problem before it is a technology problem. Without a baseline — a documented snapshot of the current situation — any positive outcome remains a feeling rather than a figure. And without figures, you cannot justify the expenditure, cannot improve the system and cannot decide whether to scale up or stop.

A second structural problem compounds this, articulated clearly by Giuseppe Di Franco, CEO of Lutech: without reliable data, integrated digital systems and adequate competencies, technology alone generates no productivity. Many Italian SMEs and professional firms still lack the digital foundations on which an AI agent can actually operate: documented processes, structured and accessible data, and integrations between the software already in use. An AI agent cannot compensate for these gaps — it amplifies them.

A word of warning: if your firm or company cannot answer these three questions — how long does this process take today? how many errors does it produce? what do those errors cost? — any investment in AI agents will begin without any ability to demonstrate a return. That is not a technical problem: it is a strategic one.

Adopting an AI agent vs being AI-driven: a decisive difference

"Using AI" and "being AI-driven" are not the same thing. Using AI means you have activated a tool — an email assistant, a text generator, a chatbot on your website — that someone in the firm uses occasionally, in a personal way, without changing how the organisation actually works. It is useful, but not transformative.

Being AI-driven means that the data produced by AI systems actively influences day-to-day operational decisions: the CRM knows which clients are at risk of leaving because an agent has analysed every interaction; deadlines are managed because an agent monitors document collection; email response priorities are assigned automatically based on urgency and client value. In this case AI is not an add-on — it is part of the process itself.

The leap between these two levels requires three things that no subscription can buy: a strategic choice about *what* to automate, the ability to measure *how well* it works, and the competency to *govern* the system over time. As Di Franco notes when discussing Italian industry, the skills shortage is not confined to technical staff — it concerns above all those responsible for guiding investment decisions. How to measure the ROI of an AI agent in your firm or company

Measuring the return on an AI agent is not complicated, but it demands discipline before implementation. The most robust method breaks down into four steps. First: choose a well-defined process — gathering documents for tax filings, following up on outstanding quotes, handling initial-contact emails — and document it as it stands today: average time, frequency, error rate, hourly cost of the people involved. This is your baseline.

Second: set a numerical objective before you begin. Not "reply to emails faster" but "reduce average first-response time from 4 hours to 30 minutes within 60 days". A measurable target makes the project evaluable and allows you to course-correct during the pilot. Third: run a limited pilot — one process, one team, one month — and collect the data systematically. Fourth: compare the results against the baseline and decide whether to scale, adjust or stop.

This approach is equally useful for understanding *when it is not worth* investing in an AI agent. If the process is already efficient, if baseline data do not exist, or if there is nobody available to supervise the agent over time, it is better to wait or to start somewhere else. What risks ungoverned AI agents introduce

A poorly governed AI agent is not simply a tool that does not work — it is a tool that can cause harm. For a professional firm the most concrete risks fall into three areas. The first is data confidentiality: an agent that accesses client emails, tax documents or legal files must operate on infrastructure that complies with the GDPR and, from 2025 onwards, with Italian Law 132/2025, which introduces explicit disclosure obligations towards clients when AI systems are used. We have written about this in detail in our article on Italian Law 132/2025 and AI in professional firms.

The second area of risk is professional liability: if an AI agent produces an incorrect analysis or a document draft containing errors and the professional sends it to the client without checking it, liability rests with the professional, not with the software. Automation bias — the tendency to trust AI output too readily and reduce manual checks — is one of the most underestimated risks in professional firms.

The third area is operational dependency: a firm that entrusts critical processes to an AI agent without a contingency plan leaves itself exposed to operational shutdown should the provider experience a failure. Strategy is not only about how AI is implemented, but also about how control is maintained if the AI stops working. For a deeper look at the specific risks AI agents pose to professional firms, see our article on AI agent risks for professional firms.

Where to start: strategy before tool

The most practical advice you can give to a firm owner or an SME manager is this: do not start by choosing the tool. Start by choosing the process. What is the activity that takes the most time away from your team without requiring complex professional judgement? Which one has the highest error rate or the most irregular follow-ups? That is the right candidate for your first AI agent.

Processes with high potential in professional firms typically include: gathering documents for tax deadlines, handling initial-contact emails, transcribing and summarising client calls, and managing outstanding quotes. Each of these is measurable, requires no autonomous professional judgement and produces data that can be tracked over time.

Once the process is chosen, return to the method described above: photograph the baseline, define the objective, run a contained pilot, measure and decide. An AI agent investment does not have to be large to be effective — it has to be targeted. As Di Franco observes when discussing industrial technology, the most important question is not "what can I automate?" but "what can this technology allow me to do that I currently cannot?" The answer to that question is worth far more than any list of product features.

Frequently asked questions

What are AI agents and why do they matter to SMEs and professional firms?

AI agents are artificial intelligence systems that execute sequences of actions autonomously to reach a goal: replying to emails, collecting documents, updating the CRM, qualifying contacts. They matter to SMEs and professional firms because development costs have fallen and current models are reliable enough for repetitive operational processes, freeing up professional time for higher-value work.

Why do so few businesses achieve concrete results from AI?

The main problem is that most AI projects begin without a measured baseline: the current situation is not documented, no numerical objective is set, and consequently no improvement can ever be demonstrated. Compounding this is the absence of reliable data and basic digital processes on which the agent can actually operate.

How do you measure the ROI of an AI agent in a firm or company?

In four steps: document the current situation (time, cost, error rate of the chosen process); define a numerical objective with a deadline before the pilot; run a contained pilot collecting data systematically; and finally compare results against the baseline to decide whether to scale or course-correct.

Where should a professional firm start when introducing AI agents?

With the process that consumes the most time without requiring complex professional judgement: document collection, initial-contact email management, call transcription, quote follow-ups. The key is to choose something measurable, limit the first pilot in time and scope, and document the baseline before starting.

What risks do AI agents introduce without a governance strategy?

The three main risks are: breach of client data confidentiality (GDPR, Italian Law 132/2025), professional liability for outputs that are used without being checked, and operational dependency on a provider without a continuity plan. A poorly governed AI agent is not merely ineffective — it can cause concrete harm and regulatory penalties.

What is the difference between adopting an AI agent and being AI-driven?

Adopting an AI tool means using it personally and occasionally without changing organisational processes. Being AI-driven means that data produced by AI systems actively shapes the day-to-day operational decisions of the entire firm or company. Reaching the second level requires strategy, measurement and governance — not just a software subscription.

How do you avoid wasting AI budget without seeing results?

By not starting with the tool but with the process. Choose an activity with high volume, low decision complexity and a documentable cost. Define the baseline and the objective before spending anything. Run a small pilot and measure it. If you do not reach the objective within the agreed timeframe, stop or adjust: that is useful information, not wasted money.

Conclusion

The 46%-versus-4% gap is not a discouraging statistic — it is a map of the territory. It tells you that the technology is available, that the appetite is there, and that the bottleneck lies elsewhere: in strategy, measurement and governance.

For Italian professional firms and SMEs, the competitive advantage will not come from adopting the most advanced AI agent. It will come from being among the few who know how to choose what to automate, measure whether it is genuinely working and keep it under control over time.

The next step is not to choose a tool. It is to answer one straightforward question: which process in your firm or company, automated with a clear strategy, would generate the most measurable value? Start there.
